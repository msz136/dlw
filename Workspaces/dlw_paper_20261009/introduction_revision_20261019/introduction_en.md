## 1. Introduction {#en-intro}

Nonlinear long-wave equations provide a setting in which dispersion, nonlinear transport and coherent wave propagation can be studied together. In two spatial dimensions, transverse variation introduces an additional question: how does the spatial geometry of a wave affect its propagation and interaction with other waves? Integrable models are particularly useful for this purpose because their exact solutions and auxiliary linear problems allow these effects to be examined analytically. They also provide demanding numerical benchmarks: a computed wave must reproduce its position and shape in a plane, together with the coupled fields that determine its evolution. The (2+1)-dimensional dispersive long wave (DLW) system considered here combines this multidimensional wave structure with an explicit bilinear representation.

Boiti, Leon and Pempinelli introduced the system as a two-spatial-dimensional extension of the dispersive long wave equation and developed its inverse spectral transform [1]. In the long-wave interpretation used in subsequent studies, the system describes wave motion in wide channels or open seas of finite depth [3,4]. We work with the normalized two-field form specified in Section 2.1, following [5]. This choice fixes the background and scaling conventions for the physical fields $u$ and $v$. The two horizontal coordinates enter the equations asymmetrically: mixed derivatives involving $y$ are coupled to transport and higher derivatives in $x$. Consequently, retaining the transverse dependence is important both for the analytical construction and for its numerical realization. Line-soliton solutions give a particularly accessible example, since their crests form oblique bands in the spatial plane and their intersections test the treatment of transverse coupling.

The analytical study of this system has developed through several complementary approaches. The original spectral formulation supplied a Bäcklund transformation, soliton solutions and a superposition formula [1]. Wang, Zhou and Li subsequently introduced a nonlinear transformation for constructing solutions [2], while Sun et al. obtained a bilinear representation and a bilinear auto-Bäcklund transformation using symbolic computation and generalized Bell polynomials [3]. These developments relate the nonlinear equations to auxiliary functions and transformation rules, making it possible to generate solutions without solving the original coupled equations directly. For discretization, this is a useful starting point: the transformation structure supplies algebraic relations that can be retained when a continuous spatial variable is replaced by a lattice.

Determinant formulas organize this solution structure for arbitrary finite numbers of interacting components. Hu, Xu and Yu constructed both Grammian and Wronskian solutions using Sato theory and Hirota's bilinear method, and related their construction to the Boussinesq–Burgers system through dimensional reduction [4]. Sheng and Yu developed Gram determinant solutions through a KP-hierarchy reduction, including solitons, line breathers and rational solutions on constant and periodic backgrounds [5]. Their continuous determinant family is the starting point of the present work. The representation is also useful computationally: the same parameters specify the wave profiles and provide explicit reference fields against which spatial and temporal errors can be measured.

Research on the associated Boiti–Leon–Pempinelli system has also continued beyond individual solution formulas. Maltseva and Popovych computed its point-symmetry pseudogroup, classified Lie reductions and enlarged exact-solution families using differential constraints and Laplace and Darboux transformations [11]. Their discussion of equivalent formulations makes the dependence on variable transformations explicit. This is relevant when comparing results across the DLW literature, where different normalizations and auxiliary variables occur. In the present setting, the corresponding issue arises after discretization: a lattice equation for auxiliary quantities must be accompanied by a definite reconstruction of the physical fields before its continuum limit or numerical accuracy can be assessed.

Integrable discretization addresses how much of this analytical structure can survive on a lattice. A Taylor-consistent difference approximation specifies a continuum limit, whereas a compatible auxiliary linear system imposes additional relations among the discrete equations. Hirota's direct method provides a systematic language for constructing such relations in terms of bilinear equations and determinant identities [16]. Zhang et al. used compatibility with bilinear Bäcklund transformations to derive semi-discrete analogues of several soliton equations, including extended KdV and KP systems, together with their Lax pairs and continuum limits [7]. In two spatial dimensions, Yu and Xu constructed a differential-difference system related to the KP and Zakharov equations and studied its soliton dynamics [8]. These examples motivate a construction in which the lattice equations, exact solutions and linear compatibility conditions are developed together.

For DLW, a further guide is the relation between its bilinear equations and those of the (2+1)-dimensional sinh-Gordon system discussed in [5]. Hu and Yu constructed integrable semi-discrete and fully discrete versions of the latter, together with determinant solutions and Bäcklund–Lax structures [6]. These connections suggest using shifts of both the lattice argument and the bilinear parameter. The task for the DLW evolution considered here is to select these shifts consistently with a lattice in $y$, while keeping $x$ and the physical time $t$ continuous. The reconstruction must then preserve the relation between the two physical fields and recover the chosen continuous normalization as the lattice spacing tends to zero.

Discrete DLW-related systems already occur in the literature. In particular, Tian et al. studied Lie symmetries and nonlocally related systems for continuous and discrete dispersive long-wave equations [12]. Their discrete system is expressed through a lattice index and two continuous variables, so a general claim that DLW has no discrete counterpart would be inappropriate. The problem addressed here is more specific: to construct a staggered semi-discrete realization of the bilinear system in Section 2.1, establish its determinant and Darboux–Lax representations, and quantify the relation between its reconstructed fields and the continuous DLW fields. This formulation also provides a common basis for comparing two nonlinear implementations of the same bilinear construction.

The relation between exact solutions at the two levels is essential to this comparison. A numerical solution of a semi-discrete system contains errors from the subsequent discretization in $x$ and time; its difference from a continuous DLW solution also reflects the change introduced by the $y$ lattice. Exact semi-discrete solutions allow the former errors to be examined, while exact continuous solutions provide a reference for the overall approximation to the PDE. Neither reference replaces the other. A continuum expansion of the reconstructed determinant solutions identifies the leading difference between these reference families and helps interpret an observed second-order trend. It also makes the analytical scope precise: convergence of a regular finite-dimensional family of exact solutions does not by itself establish stability or convergence of a numerical method for arbitrary initial data.

There is a second numerical motivation for retaining analytical structure. A uniform grid may allocate many points to weakly varying regions while resolving a narrow wave band inadequately. Moving mesh methods redistribute a fixed number of nodes through a solution-dependent density; their general framework includes the choice of a monitor, an equidistribution principle and an evolution law for the mesh [15]. Within integrable systems, conservation laws give a particularly direct connection between the evolving solution and the node distribution. Feng, Maruno and Ohta developed a self-adaptive method for the Camassa–Holm equation [13] and related moving-mesh schemes for short pulse equations to discrete reciprocal transformations and Lax pairs [14]. Feng, Sheng and Yu subsequently constructed integrable semi-discretizations and moving-mesh methods for a generalized sine-Gordon equation [9]. These studies motivate using a conservation density as part of the DLW numerical construction.

The present two-dimensional setting requires a specific adaptation of that idea. We keep a uniform lattice in $y$ and use a common set of moving $x$ nodes for all transverse layers. A density chosen separately on each layer would generally prescribe different node positions, so we average the semi-discrete conservation density and its flux across the layers. The resulting one-dimensional conservation law determines the shared node velocity through conservation of cumulative density. Positivity of the averaged density and preservation of node ordering are required for this coordinate description. This gives a mesh suited to the tensor-product representation of the fields, although it does not adapt the $y$ resolution or align an arbitrary mesh with each individual wave crest. Its numerical benefit must therefore be assessed for the wave geometries actually tested.

Our aim is to connect the integrable construction to this numerical use within one consistent formulation. The semi-discrete equations admit two nonlinear representations: the potential-elimination formulation (PE) and the potential formulation (PF). Their common origin provides matched exact solutions, while their different auxiliary variables and reconstruction steps lead to different numerical implementations. We compare both with direct finite differences applied to the continuous DLW equations. The spatial formulation, time integrator and mesh motion are varied in separate comparisons, since a Lax representation for the semi-discrete model alone does not determine the accuracy of its fully numerical implementation. The numerical study uses four one- and two-soliton tests and measures errors in both physical fields.

The main contributions are as follows.

**(i) A staggered bilinear construction and its exact solutions.** We combine a lattice in $y$ with shifts of the bilinear operator parameter. An adjacent-layer determinant identity yields Gram determinant solutions of arbitrary finite order, together with a sufficient condition for positivity of the τ functions (Sections 2.2–2.3).

**(ii) Nonlinear formulations and Darboux–Lax representations.** Eliminating or retaining an auxiliary potential yields PE and PF. We establish their physical-field reconstruction and realize the lattice shift through two first-order Darboux operators, whose compatibility gives the associated nonlinear equations (Sections 2.4 and 2.6).

**(iii) Quantitative continuum limits.** We prove second-order consistency of the reconstruction and equations. For fixed regular spectral parameters and positive τ functions on the compact sets under consideration, we establish second-order convergence of the reconstructed exact solutions and compute the leading difference between the semi-discrete and continuous exact families (Section 2.5 and Appendix A).

**(iv) Conservation-based mesh motion and numerical comparison.** We derive a shared-node moving-mesh method from the transversely averaged density and compare PE, PF and direct finite differences using exact soliton references. In the four tests considered, the moving mesh reduces the maximum absolute errors in both fields relative to the corresponding fixed-grid computations; these results concern the stated parameters, node counts and time intervals (Section 3).

Section 2 develops the semi-discrete equations, determinant solutions, nonlinear formulations, continuum limits and Darboux–Lax representations. Section 3 presents the numerical methods and comparisons, and Section 4 gives the conclusions. Appendices A–F supply the exact-family expansion and the determinant, Darboux, reconstruction, moving-mesh and time-stepping calculations.
