============================== section-5 ==============================
5. Numerical experiments

We use a one-soliton wave, a two-soliton interaction, Gaussian initial data and sinusoidal initial data to examine the physical fields computed by the three spatial formulations. Section 5.1 introduces these test problems, their continuous references and the error measures. Section 5.2 compares the fields at $T=0.01$ under common RK4 time advancement, using the initial derivative errors as a short diagnostic of the spatial approximation. Section 5.3 retains Centered-SD and compares the physical-field errors produced by three time integrators throughout the evolution. Section 5.4 examines how redistributing the shared $x$ nodes affects the same errors.

[SECTION section-5.1]

5.1 Test problems and error measures

All tests use $a=2$ and $\lambda=-2$. The one- and two-soliton references are obtained from the continuous Gram determinant solutions in Section 2.1, with $\rho_i=1$ and zero phase shifts. Table 1 lists their spectral parameters and regions.

[TABLE]
Case | Spectral pairs | Region | Periodic x interval
One-soliton | (1, 2) | R = 5 | [−10, 10)
Two-soliton | (1, 2), (4, −3) | R = 30 | [−40, 40)
[/TABLE]

The regions contain the principal wave profiles, and the computational boundaries are placed farther out. Writing the periodic interval as $[-L,L)$, we use the same interval in $y$. Errors are measured at the native field locations satisfying $|x|\le R$ and $|y|<R$. Both semi-discrete formulations place $u$ at nodes and $v$ at edge midpoints, whereas FD places both fields at nodes.

The one-soliton case contains a single oblique wave with negative pulses in both physical fields. The heatmaps show its spatial orientation, while the central sections resolve the pulse shape.

For the Gaussian and sinusoidal tests, let $z=y+2$ and $\kappa=\pi/4$. The initial fields are

$u_0(x,y)=A\sin(\kappa z)F(x),\qquad v_0(x,y)=4-3A\kappa\cos(\kappa z)F(x).$

The Gaussian test uses $A=0.05$ and the periodic profile $F(x)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}\exp[-((x+192j)/6)^2]$. The sinusoidal test uses $A=0.03$ and $F(x)=\cos(\pi x/12)$. Both are computed on the periodic interval $x\in[-96,96)$ with $y\in[-2,2]$ and lower boundary $u=0$. Their errors are measured over all $x$ nodes and native field locations with $|y|\le1.8$.

For these two initial-value problems, two linear heat equations and a Laplace transformation provide the continuous reference fields throughout $0\le t\le0.01$. The initial relation $v_0=4-3u_{0y}$ specifies the data; the reference fields subsequently evolve according to the full DLW system. Interval evaluation gives an absolute error bound of $10^{-13}$ for every initial derivative and terminal field value used in the comparisons.

The Gaussian test starts from a positive localized u profile with a sinusoidal envelope in y. The second field varies about v=4, with opposite departures from this background in the lower and upper halves of the region. Its central section remains at four.

Each method starts from the same continuous physical fields. At $T=0.01$, we measure the maximum absolute field errors at the native sites in the specified region:

$E_u(T)=\max_{\mathcal G_u}|u_h(T)-u^*(T)|,\qquad E_v(T)=\max_{\mathcal G_v}|v_h(T)-v^*(T)|.$

Here $\mathcal G_u$ and $\mathcal G_v$ contain the native locations of the two physical fields. As an initial diagnostic, $E_{u_t}$ and $E_{v_t}$ denote the analogous maximum differences between the spatially computed and continuous time derivatives at $t=0$. Bold entries identify the smallest error for each case and field.

One-soliton case

The one-soliton test provides a single localized profile in each field. All three methods reproduce these central pulse shapes. Centered-SD reduces the terminal u error relative to Endpoint-SD by a factor of 2.8 and the v error by a factor of 72. This first-field improvement develops during the coupled evolution, even though Endpoint-SD has the smaller initial u_t defect. The first equation contains the curvature of v, so the approximation of the second field also enters the evolution of the first.

5.3 Comparison of time integrators

We next retain Centered-SD on the fixed grid and vary only the time integrator. Forward Euler, classical RK4 and Crank–Nicolson use the same initial fields, the same boundary data and the same Fourier $x$ operators, with $h=\Delta x=0.1$ and $\Delta t=10^{-4}$, and each calculation is followed from $t=0$ to $T=0.01$.

At every time step $t_n$ we record the largest discrepancy in each physical field over the native locations of the region specified in Section 5.1,

$E_u^{(m)}(t_n)=\max_{\mathcal G_u}\left|u_h^{(m)}(t_n)-u^*(t_n)\right|,\qquad E_v^{(m)}(t_n)=\max_{\mathcal G_v}\left|v_h^{(m)}(t_n)-v^*(t_n)\right|.$

Here $m$ labels the time integrator. The two histories show how the largest error in each field develops, although the location of that maximum may change during the evolution.

Table 4 lists the values at $T=0.01$. The RK4 rows reproduce the Centered-SD column of Table 3, since both calculations use the same spatial scheme and the same time step.

RK4 and Crank–Nicolson are almost indistinguishable. Their largest relative difference is $7.3\times10^{-3}$, in the first field of the two-soliton case, and it is below $4.3\times10^{-4}$ in the other entries. At this step size the error is therefore controlled by the spatial approximation, and the second- and fourth-order schemes give the same accuracy.

Forward Euler changes the level of the error rather than its development. It gives a smaller $u$ error than RK4 in all four tests, by $0.8\%$, $21\%$, $82\%$ and $38\%$, and a larger $v$ error, by $3.8\%$, $8.0\%$, and factors of $8.2$ and $9.0$. These changes follow the sign of the first-order temporal defect relative to the spatial error, so the reduction in $u$ does not indicate a better resolution of that field. Halving the step moves the Euler results towards RK4 in all four tests: for the one-soliton case the errors change from $1.664\times10^{-4}$ and $8.026\times10^{-5}$ to $1.671\times10^{-4}$ and $7.880\times10^{-5}$, and for the two-soliton case from $1.420\times10^{-4}$ and $1.090\times10^{-4}$ to $1.560\times10^{-4}$ and $1.048\times10^{-4}$.

Figures 25 and 26 show the two histories for all four tests. The maximum error of each field increases monotonically and is attained at the final time for every integrator and every test. In the one-soliton, Gaussian and sinusoidal cases the growth is nearly proportional to $t$, so that the ratio $E(t)/t$ varies by less than $10\%$ over the interval; in the two-soliton case the first-field curves steepen during the last part of the calculation, where the interaction develops. The curves of RK4 and Crank–Nicolson overlap within the line width, while the Euler curve is displaced by an almost constant factor.

We retain RK4 for the mesh comparison, where a common time integrator isolates the effect of the node distribution.

[SECTION section-5.4]

5.4 Fixed and adaptive meshes

We now examine how the distribution of the shared $x$ nodes affects the computed fields. The uniform and adaptive calculations have the same node count, the same $y$ lattice, the same RK4 step $\Delta t=10^{-4}$ and the same final time. Both use the mapped centred operators of Section 4.3, so the only difference is the node distribution; those operators are local in the computational coordinate rather than Fourier multipliers, so the fixed-mesh errors below are larger than those of Table 3.

In the adaptive calculation the initial nodes equidistribute the transverse-averaged conservation density computed from the exact initial fields, and the nodes then move with the velocity of Section 4.3. The density ranges from $1.000$ to $1.150$ in the one-soliton case and from $1.000$ to $1.050$ in the two-soliton case. Figure 27 shows the resulting local spacing, normalized by the uniform spacing, at $t=0$ and $T=0.01$.

[IMG src=Workspaces/dlw_paper_20261009/time_samm_20261025/figures/mesh_spacing.png]
Figure 27. Local $x$ spacing normalized by the uniform spacing $\Delta x=0.1$, plotted at the interval midpoints of (a) the one-soliton and (b) the two-soliton case. Grey dashed: initial adaptive mesh; blue: $T=0.01$; orange dotted: uniform spacing. PDF

Nodes are concentrated where the density is large. In the one-soliton case the spacing falls to $0.90\,\Delta x$ across the wave region, and the background is stretched to $1.04\,\Delta x$; in the two-soliton case the spacing varies between $0.97\,\Delta x$ and $1.02\,\Delta x$. The initial and final distributions nearly coincide: the largest node displacement during the calculation is $9.5\times10^{-4}$ in the one-soliton case and $5.2\times10^{-4}$ in the two-soliton case. The pattern is therefore set by the initial equidistribution and adjusted only slightly by the evolution, and the total discrete mass of the averaged density changes by less than $10^{-7}$ relative.

Table 5 compares the terminal errors on the fixed and adaptive meshes and reports the relative change

$\mathcal R_f=100\left(1-\frac{E_f^{\mathrm{adaptive}}(T)}{E_f^{\mathrm{uniform}}(T)}\right)\%,\qquad f=u,v,$

so that positive values correspond to a reduction. The adaptive mesh lowers both errors in both cases: by $11.7\%$ in $u$ and $17.4\%$ in $v$ for the one-soliton case, and by $6.0\%$ and $5.6\%$ for the two-soliton case.

The reduction is already obtained from the initial distribution. Holding the equidistributed initial nodes fixed gives changes of $12.8\%$ and $16.3\%$ for the one-soliton case and $5.3\%$ and $5.8\%$ for the two-soliton case, within $1.1$ percentage points of the values in Table 5. The node motion over this short interval is therefore not the main mechanism; it slightly raises the first-field error of the one-soliton case and slightly lowers the second-field error.

The adaptive mesh lowers the error over the central region of both fields: the dominant peak of $u$ falls from $6.74\times10^{-4}$ to $5.95\times10^{-4}$, and the plateau of $v$ along the wave band falls from $1.40\times10^{-3}$ to $1.16\times10^{-3}$. In the two-soliton case the reduction is smaller, as the last row of Table 5 shows.

Within this setting the conservation density provides a useful allocation of the shared nodes. With the same node count and the same time integrator it lowers the maximum error in both physical fields of both tests, and most of the gain is obtained from the initial distribution. The improvement is limited by the small motion over the short interval and by the error being spread over the wave region rather than concentrated at isolated features.
