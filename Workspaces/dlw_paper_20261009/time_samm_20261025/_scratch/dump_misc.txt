4. Numerical formulation and linear stability

We use the physical-field equations to examine how spatial approximation and time integration affect the computed waves. Section 4.1 describes the common x operators and the three spatial formulations. In Section 4.2, we specify the initial and boundary data and introduce the time integrators used in the experiments.

[SECTION section-4.1]

4.1 Fourier differentiation and spatial formulations

We apply the same Fourier differentiation in $x$ to Endpoint-SD, Centered-SD and FD. The operators $\mathcal D_x$ and $\mathcal D_{xx}$ have multipliers $\mathrm i\xi$ and $-\xi^2$, respectively. The first-derivative multiplier is set to zero at the Nyquist frequency. Products are evaluated at the grid points. These calligraphic operators denote numerical differentiation and are distinct from Hirota's bilinear derivatives.

Both semi-discrete formulations place $u$ at nodes and $v$ at edge midpoints; FD places both fields at nodes. Write $H(u)=u^2/2+2au$, $\Delta_hq_k=(q_k-q_{k-1})/h$, $\mathcal M_-q_k=(q_k+q_{k-1})/2$ and $\delta_0q_k=(q_{k+1}-q_{k-1})/(2h)$. Recalling the physical-field equations (27)–(28), Endpoint-SD takes the form

$\begin{aligned}\Delta_h\dot u_k&=-\mathcal D_x\Delta_hH(u_k)-\mathcal D_{xx}v_k,\\\dot v_k&=-\mathcal D_x[(\mathcal M_-u_k+2a-2h)v_k+hv_k^2/4-4\mathcal M_-u_k-h(\Delta_hu_k)^2/4]-\mathcal D_{xx}\Delta_hu_k.\end{aligned}$

For Centered-SD, the field definition in Section 3.2 gives $v_k=\Delta_hu_k+2(\omega_k+\omega_{k-1})/h$. We retain this relation in the numerical formulation. The equations for the time derivatives are

$\begin{aligned}\Delta_h\dot u_k&=-\mathcal D_x\Delta_h[H(u_k)+\omega_k^2/2-h\omega_k]-\mathcal D_{xx}v_k,\\\dot\omega_k&=\mathcal D_{xx}\omega_k-\mathcal D_x[(u_k+2a)\omega_k-hu_k],\\\dot v_k&=\Delta_h\dot u_k+\frac2h(\dot\omega_k+\dot\omega_{k-1}).\end{aligned}$

The first equation is summed from the prescribed lower value of $\dot u$. Advancing $(u,\omega)$ and recovering $v$ through the field relation implements the physical-field equations of Proposition 3.2. The same differentiated relation supplies $\dot v$ when measuring the initial spatial error.

For comparison, direct discretization of the continuous DLW system gives the FD baseline,

$\begin{aligned}\Delta_h\dot u_k&=-\mathcal D_x\Delta_hH(u_k)-\mathcal D_{xx}\mathcal M_-v_k,\\\dot v_k&=-\mathcal D_x[(u_k+2a)v_k-4u_k]-\mathcal D_{xx}\delta_0u_k.\end{aligned}$

These equations use the same Fourier operators and lower derivative data as the two semi-discrete formulations. The location of the physical fields and the discrete nonlinear fluxes determine their different spatial approximations.

[/SECTION section-4.1]

[SECTION section-4.2]

4.2 Initial and boundary data and time integration

We specify the initial and boundary data, then describe how forward Euler, classical RK4 and Crank–Nicolson advance the physical fields in our numerical experiments. Each spatial formulation samples the same continuous reference fields at its native lattice locations. Endpoint-SD and FD advance the state $z=(u_k,v_k)_k$; Centered-SD advances $z=(u_k,\omega_k)_k$ and recovers $v$ through the field relation in Section 4.1. At each time stage, the current state and the prescribed boundary data determine the derivatives required by the time integrator.

For Centered-SD, the initial lower auxiliary value is $\omega_0=h(v^*-u_y^*)/4$, evaluated at the lower node. The remaining values follow from the linear reconstruction (34),

$\omega_k=\frac h2(v_k-\Delta_hu_k)-\omega_{k-1}.$

This recurrence reproduces the prescribed physical field $v$ on every internal edge. During evolution, the lower auxiliary value follows the same $\omega$ equation as the interior values. The lower value of $u_t$ is supplied by the continuous reference solution at every stage.

Endpoint-SD uses the prescribed lower derivative and the continuous lower ghost value of $u$ in its backward lattice relations. For FD, the lower data are supplied in the same way, while the upper ghost value extrapolates the departure from the continuous reference. Writing $e_k=u_k-u_k^*$, this closure is $u_{N+1}=u_{N+1}^*+3e_N-3e_{N-1}+e_{N-2}$. The reference values are evaluated at the current stage time. Fourier differentiation acts on a periodic $x$ interval extending beyond the principal wave profiles; the products are evaluated at the nodes without filtering, dealiasing or artificial viscosity.

Writing the resulting evolution equations as $\dot z=F(t,z)$, forward Euler advances the state by $z^{n+1}=z^n+\Delta t F(t_n,z^n)$. Classical RK4 uses four stage derivatives,

$\begin{aligned}K_1&=F(t_n,z^n),&K_2&=F(t_n+\Delta t/2,z^n+\Delta t K_1/2),\\K_3&=F(t_n+\Delta t/2,z^n+\Delta t K_2/2),&K_4&=F(t_n+\Delta t,z^n+\Delta t K_3),\\z^{n+1}&=z^n+\frac{\Delta t}{6}(K_1+2K_2+2K_3+K_4).\end{aligned}$

Crank–Nicolson determines the next state from

$z^{n+1}=z^n+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\bigl[F(t_n,z^n)+F(t_{n+1},z^{n+1})\bigr].$

The implicit step uses an Euler prediction followed by fixed-point iteration, with a stopping criterion of $10^{-12}$ for the maximum absolute equation residual. The spatial-formulation comparisons in Section 5.2 use RK4 with $\Delta t=10^{-4}$ and final time $T=0.01$. In every comparison, errors are measured in the physical fields $u,v$.

The three methods share the same spatial coordinates and linear height scale within each panel. The display window is $[-12,12]$; the table uses the full periodic interval $[-96,96)$.

The two soliton tests show the same componentwise comparison despite their different wave structures: Centered-SD improves both fields relative to Endpoint-SD and gives the smallest error in the second field. The Gaussian and sinusoidal tests retain this second-field advantage at smaller absolute error levels. The resulting physical-field comparisons connect the choice of spatial formulation with the accuracy of the computed waves.
