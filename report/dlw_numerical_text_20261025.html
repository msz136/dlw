One-soliton case

The one-soliton test provides a single localized profile in each field. All three methods reproduce these central pulse shapes. Centered-SD reduces the terminal u error relative to Endpoint-SD by a factor of 2.8 and the v error by a factor of 72. This first-field improvement develops during the coupled evolution, even though Endpoint-SD has the smaller initial u_t defect. The first equation contains the curvature of v, so the approximation of the second field also enters the evolution of the first.

Two-soliton case

The two-soliton test retains the stronger wave of the preceding case and adds a second profile of different polarity in u. Centered-SD again improves both physical fields relative to Endpoint-SD: the u error is reduced from 4.942 × 10⁻⁴ to 1.792 × 10⁻⁴, and the v error from 7.076 × 10⁻³ to 1.009 × 10⁻⁴. The corresponding factors, 2.8 and 70, are close to those of the one-soliton test. The heatmaps and surfaces show how these errors extend across the two interacting wave bands.

Gaussian case

The Gaussian profile remains close to its continuous reference after the short evolution. Endpoint-SD gives the smallest u error, whereas Centered-SD gives the smallest v error, 6.967 × 10⁻⁹. The latter is approximately 3.2 times smaller than FD and 87 times smaller than Endpoint-SD. The exact second field equals four along y=0; its errors away from this symmetry line are visible in the two-dimensional maps and the surface views.

Sinusoidal case

The sinusoidal test gives the same ordering as the Gaussian test. Centered-SD produces a v error of 4.706 × 10⁻⁹, approximately 3.5 times smaller than FD and 60 times smaller than Endpoint-SD, while its u error lies between the other two methods. One spatial period is shown, making the repeated structure of the wave and its error visible in each representation.

The two soliton tests show the same componentwise comparison despite their different wave structures: Centered-SD improves both fields relative to Endpoint-SD and gives the smallest error in the second field. The Gaussian and sinusoidal tests retain this second-field advantage at smaller absolute error levels. The resulting physical-field comparisons connect the choice of spatial formulation with the accuracy of the computed waves.
