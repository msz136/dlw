\begin{abstract}
Exact solutions of the \((2+1)\)-dimensional dispersive long-wave (DLW) system provide a detailed description of nonlinear wave propagation and interaction. Extending these solutions to a spatial lattice brings their integrable structure into a discrete setting and raises the question of how the lattice changes the physical waves. In this work, we develop two related integrable semi-discretizations to study this connection between lattice construction and wave approximation. Starting from the Hirota–Miwa equation, a Miwa expansion gives an endpoint formulation (Endpoint-SD). A half-step parameter shift and a staggered placement of the \(\tau\) functions then yield a centered formulation (Centered-SD). Both admit explicit Gram determinant solutions and nonlinear physical-field equations with compatible Darboux–Lax representations, allowing the effects of lattice placement to be studied within a common integrable framework. To quantify these effects, we first examine how the equations and their exact wave fields approach their continuous counterparts. Endpoint-SD is second-order consistent in the first field equation and first-order consistent in the second, while Centered-SD achieves second-order consistency in both. Under suitable regularity and positivity assumptions, we further establish second-order approximation of the exact Centered-SD wave fields. Together with the leading correction to the Endpoint-SD equation residual, this relates the lattice construction to its continuum approximation. These analytical comparisons provide a basis for numerical experiments with soliton, Gaussian and sinusoidal profiles. Through these experiments, we show how lattice placement, wave profile and field reconstruction jointly influence computational accuracy, with the consistency analysis helping to interpret the behavior at finite lattice spacing. Building on this spatial comparison, we then examine how the errors develop during numerical evolution under different time integrators. The semi-discrete structure also offers a way to improve the resolution of the waves. Specifically, we establish a conservation law and use it to construct a self-adaptive moving mesh, improving the accuracy of both fields in the soliton tests at a fixed node count. These results clarify how the arrangement of an integrable lattice and the reconstruction of its physical fields influence the approximation of continuous waves. By tracing these choices from the bilinear equations to the evolving field errors, the study provides a basis for understanding and improving numerical accuracy within a common integrable framework.
\end{abstract}

{\bfseries Keywords}: Dispersive long-wave system; Integrable discretization; Bilinear method; Self-adaptive moving mesh method; Consistency analysis

\section{Introduction}

The propagation and interaction of shallow-water waves have long attracted interest in the study of nonlinear wave phenomena. Nonlinearity and dispersion shape the evolving wave profiles, and their balance can support coherent waves that persist during propagation. Nonlinear long-wave equations describe these effects in a reduced form, allowing wave motion to be studied without retaining the full complexity of the underlying fluid equations. Within this class, integrable models provide exact solutions through which individual waves and their interactions can be examined, together with bilinear formulations and Lax representations that describe the structures governing their evolution. These analytical results also provide reference solutions for numerical computation. Representing the waves on a spatial lattice introduces choices that affect both their approximation and the structure of the governing equations. Integrable discretization brings these questions together by constructing discrete equations alongside their exact solutions and continuum limits.

The \((2+1)\)-dimensional dispersive long-wave (DLW) system extends the dispersive long-wave equation to two spatial dimensions [1] and has been studied in connection with wave motion in wide channels and open seas of finite depth [3,4]. Since its introduction by Boiti, Leon and Pempinelli, its solutions have been investigated through several related analytical approaches. The inverse spectral transform, Bäcklund transformation and superposition formula established in the original work [1] were followed by nonlinear transformations [2] and bilinear methods [3], providing further ways to construct wave solutions and understand their relations. Determinant representations give these solution families an explicit form. Hu, Xu and Yu developed Grammian and Wronskian solutions [4], while Sheng and Yu used a KP-hierarchy reduction to obtain Gram determinant solutions describing solitons, line breathers and rational waves on constant and periodic backgrounds [5]. Their bilinear formulation and determinant construction provide the continuous starting point for the present study.

The bilinear relations underlying these solutions also offer a starting point for introducing a spatial lattice [15]. Bäcklund transformations can relate neighbouring lattice values, while expansions of the Hirota–Miwa equation [17,18] connect discrete bilinear relations to equations with continuous variables. These approaches have been developed for semi-discrete soliton equations [7] and extended to systems with two spatial dimensions [8]. For the DLW system, a further connection comes from its bilinear relation to the \((2+1)\)-dimensional sinh-Gordon equation [5], whose integrable discretizations admit determinant solutions and Bäcklund–Lax structures [6]. Together with earlier studies of discrete DLW systems [11], these developments provide a basis for carrying continuous wave families onto a spatial lattice. They also invite a closer examination of what this passage means for the physical waves, whose approximation depends on both the lattice equations and the reconstruction of the physical fields.

Building on this work, we wish to understand how the preservation of integrable structure can contribute to the approximation and computation of continuous waves. We seek a semi-discrete setting in which the bilinear equations, exact solutions, and reconstructed physical fields can be studied together, allowing us to trace the effect of introducing a lattice from the analytical construction to the resulting wave profiles. In particular, we ask how different lattice arrangements within a common integrable framework affect the accuracy of the physical fields, and how the available analytical structure can guide their numerical computation. These questions connect the construction of integrable lattices with the broader task of understanding and improving numerical approximations of nonlinear waves.

To investigate these questions, we construct two integrable semi-discretizations of the DLW system from the Hirota–Miwa equation. The endpoint construction, denoted by Endpoint-SD, follows from a Miwa expansion. Starting from this lattice, we introduce a shift of the parameter reference and a staggered placement of the \(\tau\) functions to obtain the centered construction, denoted by Centered-SD. We establish their Gram determinant solutions and Darboux–Lax representations, and show how the lattice arrangement enters the reconstructed physical-field equations. Endpoint-SD has second-order consistency in the first equation and first-order consistency in the second, whereas Centered-SD achieves second-order consistency in both. We derive the leading correction to the Endpoint-SD equation residual and establish second-order approximation of the exact Centered-SD wave fields under the stated assumptions. These results show how two constructions with shared integrable structures can produce different approximation properties, and identify the role of lattice placement and field reconstruction in determining those properties.

The exact solutions provide benchmarks for examining how the analytical approximation properties are reflected in numerical computation. We compare initial time-derivative errors and follow the physical-field errors through time on soliton, Gaussian, and sinusoidal backgrounds. These experiments, together with the spatial error analysis, help explain how the formulation and time integrator affect the accuracy of the evolving waves. The semi-discrete conservation law also supports a shared-node moving mesh, which reduces the errors in both fields at a fixed node count in the soliton tests. Independent initial perturbations extend the study to the development of coupled responses within each formulation. Through these investigations, we seek to clarify how the analytical structure of an integrable semi-discretization can help us understand numerical behavior and improve the computation of nonlinear waves.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the continuous DLW bilinear system and develops the Endpoint-SD and Centered-SD constructions, together with their Gram determinant solutions and positivity conditions. Section 3 reconstructs the nonlinear physical-field equations, establishes their Darboux–Lax representations, and examines the continuum limits of the equations and exact wave fields. Building on these formulations, Section 4 describes the spatial discretization, initial and boundary data, time integration, and conservation-based moving mesh. Section 5 presents the numerical experiments on physical-field accuracy, mesh adaptation, and the evolution of independent initial perturbations. Section 6 summarizes the main findings.

\section{Integrable bilinear semi-discrete formulations and exact solutions}

In this section, we develop a bilinear lattice description of the DLW system that connects the continuous equations, the arrangement of the \(\tau\) functions and the construction of exact solutions. In Section 2.1, we recall the continuous system and its bilinear representation, fixing the parameters and the transformation to the physical fields. In Section 2.2, we derive an unreduced bilinear lattice from the Hirota–Miwa equation and establish a unified Gram determinant family through an adjacent-layer identity. Section 2.3 specializes this construction to the endpoint and centered formulations, compares their bilinear consistency, and gives a positive real solution class.


\subsection{The continuous DLW system and its bilinear representation}

We begin with the continuous DLW system and its bilinear representation. Specifically, we seek a description in which the physical fields and their explicit solutions can be obtained from a common pair of \(\tau\) functions. With real parameters \(a\) and \(\lambda\), the continuous equations are 
\paperdisplay{u_{yt}+v_{xx}+[(u+2a)u_y]_x=0,\qquad v_t+u_{xxy}+[(u+2a)v+2\lambda u]_x=0.}{\tag{1}}

For positive, sufficiently smooth functions \(f,g\), we use the transformation 
\paperdisplay{u=2\partial_x\log(f/g),\qquad v=2\partial_{xy}\log(fg).}{\tag{2}}
Hirota's bilinear derivatives are defined by 
\paperdisplay{D_x^mD_y^nD_t^\ell f\cdot g= \left.(\partial_x-\partial_{x'})^m(\partial_y-\partial_{y'})^n (\partial_t-\partial_{t'})^\ell f(x,y,t)g(x',y',t')\right|_{(x',y',t')=(x,y,t)}.}{\tag{3}}
The bilinear equations are 
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}&\left(D_x^2+D_t+2aD_x\right)f\cdot g=0,\\&\left(D_x^2D_y+D_tD_y+2aD_xD_y+2\lambda D_x\right)f\cdot g=0.\end{aligned}}{\tag{4}}
Their logarithmic expansion, followed by (2), gives the continuous field equations (1). In the work of Sheng and Yu [5], we have the bilinear representation above and the continuous Gram determinant solutions given below.

For the lattice construction, we fix \(\lambda=-2\). This replaces \(2\lambda u\) by \(-4u\) in the second field equation and \(2\lambda D_x\) by \(-4D_x\) in the second bilinear equation. Thus 
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}&\left(D_x^2+D_t+2aD_x\right)f\cdot g=0,\\&\left(D_x^2D_y+D_tD_y+2aD_xD_y-4D_x\right)f\cdot g=0.\end{aligned}}{\tag{5}}
The first relation describes the \(x,t\) dependence on each fixed-\(y\) slice, while the second connects these slices through the \(y\) derivative. We therefore consider a lattice in \(y\), keeping \(x\) and \(t\) continuous.

The continuous Gram family is denoted by \(\tau_n^{(0)}\), with the superscript \((0)\) identifying the continuous reference family. It can be written as 
\paperdisplay{\tau_n^{(0)}=\det_{1\le i,j\le N}\left[ \delta_{ij}+\frac{\rho_i}{p_i+q_j} \left(-\frac{p_i-a}{q_j+a}\right)^n e^{\xi_i+\eta_j}\right], \qquad f=\tau_1^{(0)},\quad g=\tau_0^{(0)},}{\tag{6}}
where 
\paperdisplay{\xi_i=p_ix-p_i^2t+\frac{y}{p_i-a},\qquad \eta_j=q_jx+q_j^2t+\frac{y}{q_j+a}.}{\tag{7}}
The parameters are chosen so that the denominators are nonzero. On regions where \(\tau_1^{(0)}\) and \(\tau_0^{(0)}\) are positive, (2) gives smooth real physical fields. These \(\tau\) functions solve the bilinear equations (5).


\subsection{Bilinear semi-discrete construction and Gram determinant solutions}

We seek a lattice description of the bilinear system (5) in which the transverse variable is discrete and the longitudinal variable and time remain continuous. To construct it, we start from the Hirota–Miwa equation and take a continuous limit in two lattice directions. The remaining directions will supply the pairings between neighbouring lattice functions. We then give a unified Gram determinant family for this lattice and establish its bilinear identities by a direct adjacent-layer calculation.

Following the inverse-spacing convention in [15], let \(T_\alpha,T_\beta,T_\gamma\) denote commuting shifts, with \(\tau_\alpha=T_\alpha\tau\) and \(\tau_{\alpha\beta}=T_\alpha T_\beta\tau\). The Hirota–Miwa equation [17,18] is
\paperdisplay{(\gamma-\beta)\tau_\alpha\tau_{\beta\gamma} +(\alpha-\gamma)\tau_\beta\tau_{\alpha\gamma} +(\beta-\alpha)\tau_\gamma\tau_{\alpha\beta}=0.}{\tag{8}}

The Hirota–Miwa equation holds for every triple of distinct directions in the multidimensional lattice. We take continuous limits in the directions labelled by \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), keeping \(\gamma\) fixed. For integer coordinates \(\ell_1,\ell_2\) in the first two directions, the Miwa transformation [18] is

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}&
X_m=X_m^{(0)}+\frac{\ell_1}{m\alpha^m}+\frac{\ell_2}{m\beta^m},\qquad m\ge1,\\&
[\varepsilon]=\left(\varepsilon,\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2},\frac{\varepsilon^3}{3},\ldots\right),\qquad
T_\alpha\tau(\boldsymbol X)=\tau(\boldsymbol X+[\alpha^{-1}]).
\end{aligned}}{\tag{9}}
The shift \(T_\beta\) acts in the same way with \(\alpha\) replaced by \(\beta\). Thus a lattice step translates all hierarchy variables simultaneously. In particular,

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}
T_\alpha&=\exp\!\left(\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{\alpha^{-m}}{m}\partial_{X_m}\right),\\
T_\alpha\tau&=\tau+\alpha^{-1}\tau_{X_1}
+\frac{\alpha^{-2}}2\left(\tau_{X_1X_1}+\tau_{X_2}\right)+O(\alpha^{-3}).
\end{aligned}}{\tag{10}}

We use the successive continuum limits described in [19, equations (1.7)–(1.9)], expressed here in the inverse-parameter convention of (8). First multiply (8) by \(\beta^{-1}\), expand the \(\beta\) shifts using (10), and compare the coefficient of \(\beta^{-1}\). The constant terms cancel, giving

\paperdisplay{D_{X_1}\tau_\alpha\cdot\tau_\gamma
=(\alpha-\gamma)\left(\tau_\alpha\tau_\gamma-\tau\tau_{\alpha\gamma}\right).}{\tag{11}}

Next let \(\alpha^{-1}\to0\). Since the shifts commute, (10) applies to both \(\tau_\alpha=T_\alpha\tau\) and \(\tau_{\alpha\gamma}=T_\alpha\tau_\gamma\). Substituting these expansions into (11), the terms of order \(\alpha^0\) cancel, and the remaining relation is

\paperdisplay{0=\frac1{2\alpha}\left(D_{X_1}^2-D_{X_2}+2\gamma D_{X_1}\right)
\tau\cdot\tau_\gamma+O(\alpha^{-2}).}{\tag{12}}
The coefficient of \(\alpha^{-1}\) therefore yields

\paperdisplay{\left(D_{X_1}^2-D_{X_2}+2\gamma D_{X_1}\right)
\tau\cdot T_\gamma\tau=0.}{\tag{13}}
These expansions are understood as formal power series in the inverse lattice parameters. For the Gram solutions below, the shift factors are analytic near \(\alpha^{-1}=\beta^{-1}=0\). Equation (13) retains one discrete direction and two continuous variables. The higher hierarchy variables \(X_m\), \(m\ge3\), are held fixed when identifying the DLW variables.

We now realize two retained directions using the Gram family below. On each nonconstant Gram entry, the shift \(T_\gamma\) has multiplier \((\gamma+q_j+a)/(\gamma-p_i+a)\). Under the spectral conditions stated below, this multiplier and its inverse have finite limits at \(\gamma=0\), giving \(-(q_j+a)/(p_i-a)\). This continuation defines \(T_0\) for this family. Choose the two retained parameters as \(0,d\), with integer coordinates \(s,r\), respectively, and set

\paperdisplay{n=r+s,\qquad k=-r.}{\tag{14}}
Increasing \(s\) advances only \(n\), whereas increasing \(r\) advances \(n\) and decreases \(k\). Consequently,

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}&
T_0\tau_n(k)=\tau_{n+1}(k),\qquad
T_d\tau_n(k)=\tau_{n+1}(k-1),\\&
T_0T_d^{-1}\tau_n(k)=\tau_n(k+1).
\end{aligned}}{\tag{15}}
The last relation identifies a forward step in the physical lattice with a composition of the retained shifts. Applying (13) in the two directions gives

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}&(D_{X_1}^2-D_{X_2})\tau_n(k)\cdot\tau_{n+1}(k)=0,\\&(D_{X_1}^2-D_{X_2}+2dD_{X_1})\tau_n(k)\cdot\tau_{n+1}(k-1)=0.\end{aligned}}{\tag{16}}

To identify the continuous variables with those of the DLW bilinear operator, set
\paperdisplay{X_1=x-2bt,\qquad X_2=-t,\qquad f_k=\tau_0(k),\quad g_k=\tau_1(k).}{\tag{17}}
The chain rule gives \(D_x=D_{X_1}\) and \(D_t=-2bD_{X_1}-D_{X_2}\), so that \(D_{X_1}^2-D_{X_2}=\left(D_x^2+D_t+2bD_x\right)\). Taking \(d=-h\), with \(h>0\), converts the two relations at \(n=0\) into the following lattice.

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}&\left(D_x^2+D_t+2bD_x\right)f_k\cdot g_k=0,\\&\left(D_x^2+D_t+(2b-2h)D_x\right)f_k\cdot g_{k-1}=0.\end{aligned}}{\tag{18}}
The parameter \(b\) is left free at this stage. The index \(k\) labels the transverse lattice, while \(n\) labels adjacent members of the auxiliary \(\tau\) sequence. We select \(n=0,1\) without imposing a pseudo two-reduction, so the \(x\) and \(t\) flows remain independent.

The two retained directions have spectral multipliers \(-(q_j+a)/(p_i-a)\) and \((q_j+a-h)/(a-h-p_i)\). Under the change of indices (14), their combined contribution is
\paperdisplay{\left(-\frac{q_j+a}{p_i-a}\right)^n\left[\frac{-(q_j+a)/(p_i-a)}{(q_j+a-h)/(a-h-p_i)}\right]^k=\left(-\frac{q_j+a}{p_i-a}\right)^n\left(\frac{1+h/(p_i-a)}{1-h/(q_j+a)}\right)^k.}{\tag{19}}

The first two Miwa variables under (17) contribute the phase \((p_i+q_j)[x-2(b-a)t]+(q_j^2-p_i^2)t\). Moreover, \(\log \left(\frac{1+h/(p_i-a)}{1-h/(q_j+a)}\right)=h((p_i-a)^{-1}+(q_j+a)^{-1})+O(h^2)\), so for \(kh=y\) the spatial factor tends to \(e^{y((p_i-a)^{-1}+(q_j+a)^{-1})}\).

Let \(N\ge1\), \(h>0\), and let \(a,b,c_{ij},\alpha_i,\beta_j,p_i,q_j\) be real constants satisfying
\paperdisplay{(p_i-a)(q_j+a)(p_i+q_j)(p_i-a+h)(q_j+a-h)\ne0.}{\tag{20}}
For integer indices \(n,k\), we define the common Gram family by
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}\tau_n^{(b)}(k)&=\det_{1\le i,j\le N}\Bigg[c_{ij}+\frac{\alpha_i\beta_j}{p_i+q_j}\left(-\frac{q_j+a}{p_i-a}\right)^n\\&\qquad\qquad\times\left(\frac{1+h/(p_i-a)}{1-h/(q_j+a)}\right)^k\exp\!\big[(p_i+q_j)[x-2(b-a)t]+(q_j^2-p_i^2)t\big]\Bigg].\end{aligned}}{\tag{21}}

To verify this family directly, we first establish the adjacent-layer identity. Its proof separates the row and column factors of the Gram entries, expresses a layer shift as a rank-one update, and reduces the bilinear relation to a differential equation for a ratio of two determinants.

\begin{lem}
Let \(C=(c_{ij})\) be a constant matrix. For fixed parameters \(p_i,q_j,a,b,\sigma\), assume \((p_i+q_j)(p_i+b-a-\sigma)(q_j+a-b+\sigma)\ne0\). For each \(\ell\in\mathbb Z\), define
\paperdisplay{\mathcal T_\ell(\sigma)=\det_{1\le i,j\le N}\!\left[c_{ij}+\frac{a_i b_j}{p_i+q_j}\left(-\frac{p_i+b-a-\sigma}{q_j+a-b+\sigma}\right)^\ell e^{(p_i+q_j)[x-2(b-a)t]+(q_j^2-p_i^2)t}\right].}{\tag{22}}
Here \(a_i,b_j\) are independent of \(x,t\); they may depend on a fixed lattice index. Then
\paperdisplay{\left(D_x^2+D_t+2\sigma D_x\right)\mathcal T_{\ell+1}(\sigma)\cdot\mathcal T_\ell(\sigma)=0.}{\tag{23}}
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
Write the matrix at layer \(\ell\) as \(M=C+(r_i c_j/(p_i+q_j))\), where the entries of the column vectors \(r,c\) are
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}r_i&=a_i[-(p_i+b-a-\sigma)]^\ell e^{(p_i+b-a)x-(p_i+b-a)^2t},\\c_j&=b_j(q_j+a-b+\sigma)^{-\ell}e^{(q_j+a-b)x+(q_j+a-b)^2t}.\end{aligned}}{\tag{24}}
Define the vector \(d\) by \(d_j=c_j/(q_j+a-b+\sigma)\). Differentiation and the change of layer give the component relations
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}
(r_i)_x&=(p_i+b-a)r_i,&\quad(r_i)_t&=-(p_i+b-a)^2r_i,\\
(c_j)_x&=(q_j+a-b)c_j,&\quad(c_j)_t&=(q_j+a-b)^2c_j,\\
(M_x)_{ij}&=r_ic_j,&\quad(M_t)_{ij}&=[q_j-p_i+2(a-b)]r_ic_j,\\
(M_{\ell+1}-M)_{ij}&=-\frac{r_ic_j}{q_j+a-b+\sigma}.
\end{aligned}}{\tag{25}}
In vector form, these identities read \(M_x=rc^{\mathsf T}\), \(M_t=-r_xc^{\mathsf T}+rc_x^{\mathsf T}\), and \(M_{\ell+1}=M-rd^{\mathsf T}\). The last relation follows entrywise from
\paperdisplay{-\frac{p_i+b-a-\sigma}{q_j+a-b+\sigma}-1=-\frac{p_i+q_j}{q_j+a-b+\sigma}.}{\tag{26}}

First suppose that \(M\) is invertible. Define
\paperdisplay{\kappa=c^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r,\qquad
\zeta=d^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r,\qquad
\psi=\frac{\mathcal T_{\ell+1}}{\mathcal T_\ell}=1-\zeta.}{\tag{27}}
The matrix determinant lemma gives the last equality, and Jacobi's formula gives \(\kappa=\mathcal T_{\ell,x}/\mathcal T_\ell\). The inverse matrix and the column factors satisfy
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}&
(M^{-1})_x=-M^{-1}rc^{\mathsf T}M^{-1},\\&
(M^{-1})_t=M^{-1}r_xc^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}-M^{-1}rc_x^{\mathsf T}M^{-1},\\&
d_x=c-\sigma d,\qquad d_t=c_x-\sigma c+\sigma^2d,\qquad r_t=-r_{xx}.
\end{aligned}}{\tag{28}}

Differentiating the two scalar products in (27) and using (28) yields
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}&
\kappa_x=c_x^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r+c^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r_x-\kappa^2,\\&
\zeta_x=\kappa(1-\zeta)-\sigma\zeta+d^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r_x,\\&
\partial_x(d^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r_x)
=(c^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r_x)(1-\zeta)-\sigma d^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r_x
+d^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r_{xx},\\&
\zeta_t=(c_x^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r)(1-\zeta)-\sigma\kappa+\sigma^2\zeta
-d^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r_{xx}+\kappa d^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r_x.
\end{aligned}}{\tag{29}}
Differentiate the second relation in (29) once more in \(x\) and add the fourth relation. The terms involving \(d^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r_{xx}\) cancel. Substituting the second relation for \(d^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r_x\) and collecting the remaining terms gives
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}
\zeta_{xx}+\zeta_t+2\sigma\zeta_x
&=\left(\kappa_x+c_x^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r+c^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r_x-\kappa^2\right)(1-\zeta)\\
&=2\kappa_x(1-\zeta),
\end{aligned}}{\tag{30}}
where the last equality uses the first relation in (29). Consequently, \(\psi=1-\zeta\) satisfies
\paperdisplay{\psi_{xx}+\psi_t+2\sigma\psi_x+2\kappa_x\psi=0.}{\tag{31}}

For \(f=\psi g\), expansion of the bilinear operator yields
\paperdisplay{\frac{\left(D_x^2+D_t+2\sigma D_x\right)f\cdot g}{g^2}
=\psi_{xx}+\psi_t+2\sigma\psi_x+2\left(\frac{g_x}{g}\right)_x\psi.}{\tag{32}}
Taking \(g=\mathcal T_\ell\) and \(f=\mathcal T_{\ell+1}\) identifies (31) with (23). The identity also holds at singular matrices. Indeed, replace \(C\) by \(C+\epsilon I\). At any fixed \((x,t)\), the matrix \(M+\epsilon I\) is invertible for all but finitely many \(\epsilon\), so the bilinear residual vanishes at those values. This residual is a polynomial in \(\epsilon\), hence vanishes identically. Setting \(\epsilon=0\) completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{theo}
Under (20), let \(\tau_n^{(b)}(k)\) be defined by (21). Then \(f_k=\tau_0^{(b)}(k)\) and \(g_k=\tau_1^{(b)}(k)\) satisfy the two bilinear equations (18).
\end{theo}

\begin{proof}
We apply Lemma 2.1 at a fixed lattice site \(k\), with
\paperdisplay{a_i(k)=\alpha_i\left(1+\frac{h}{p_i-a}\right)^k,\qquad b_j(k)=\beta_j\left(1-\frac{h}{q_j+a}\right)^{-k}.}{\tag{33}}
With (33), the zero-layer phase and amplitude agree with (21). Write the corresponding determinant in the lemma as \(\mathcal T_\ell(k;\sigma)\). At \(\sigma=b\), its inverse layer multiplier is \(-(q_j+a)/(p_i-a)\), and hence
\paperdisplay{\mathcal T_0(k;b)=\tau_0^{(b)}(k)=f_k,\qquad\mathcal T_{-1}(k;b)=\tau_1^{(b)}(k)=g_k.}{\tag{34}}
The inverse layer \(\ell=-1\) supplies the factor \(-(q_j+a)/(p_i-a)\), so the discrete pair in (34) uses \(f_k=\tau_0^{(b)}(k)\) and \(g_k=\tau_1^{(b)}(k)\). The continuous pair in (6) uses the reciprocal layer factor and the assignment \(f=\tau_1^{(0)},g=\tau_0^{(0)}\). Taking \(\ell=-1\) and \(\sigma=b\) in (23) gives \(\left(D_x^2+D_t+2bD_x\right)f_k\cdot g_k=0\).

For the second pairing, the zero layer is independent of \(\sigma\), so \(\mathcal T_0(k;b-h)=f_k\). The inverse layer factor at \(\sigma=b-h\) obeys
\paperdisplay{-\frac{q_j+a-h}{p_i-a+h}\left(\frac{1+h/(p_i-a)}{1-h/(q_j+a)}\right)^k=-\frac{q_j+a}{p_i-a}\left(\frac{1+h/(p_i-a)}{1-h/(q_j+a)}\right)^{k-1}.}{\tag{35}}
It follows entrywise that \(\mathcal T_{-1}(k;b-h)=\tau_1^{(b)}(k-1)=g_{k-1}\). Applying (23) with \(\ell=-1\) and \(\sigma=b-h\) therefore gives \(\left(D_x^2+D_t+(2b-2h)D_x\right)f_k\cdot g_{k-1}=0\), proving the theorem.
\end{proof}


\subsection{Endpoint and centered semi-discretizations}

The lattice (18) and its Gram family (21) depend on the free parameter \(b\). We now compare them with the continuous DLW system at fixed \(a\). Choosing \(b\) and assigning the two \(\tau\) functions to transverse positions gives the endpoint and centered formulations.

\noindent\textbf{Definition 2.1 (Endpoint-SD).} Set \(b=a\) and place both \(f_k^{\mathrm E}\) and \(g_k^{\mathrm E}\) at \(y_k=kh\). This gives the endpoint-based semi-discretization, abbreviated Endpoint-SD,
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}&\left(D_x^2+D_t+2aD_x\right)f_k^{\mathrm E}\cdot g_k^{\mathrm E}=0,\\&\left(D_x^2+D_t+(2a-2h)D_x\right)f_k^{\mathrm E}\cdot g_{k-1}^{\mathrm E}=0.\end{aligned}}{\tag{36}}
The endpoint tau functions are obtained from the determinant family through
\paperdisplay{f_k^{\mathrm E}=\tau_0^{\mathrm E}(k),\qquad g_k^{\mathrm E}=\tau_1^{\mathrm E}(k).}{\tag{37}}
Substitution of \(b=a\) in (21) gives
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}\tau_n^{\mathrm E}(k)&=\det_{1\le i,j\le N}\Bigg[c_{ij}+\frac{\alpha_i\beta_j}{p_i+q_j}\left(-\frac{q_j+a}{p_i-a}\right)^n\\&\qquad\qquad\times\left(\frac{1+h/(p_i-a)}{1-h/(q_j+a)}\right)^k\exp\!\big[(p_i+q_j)x+(q_j^2-p_i^2)t\big]\Bigg].\end{aligned}}{\tag{38}}
By Theorem 2.1, the functions in (37) satisfy (36).

To examine consistency, define 
\paperdisplay{\Phi(s)=\left(D_x^2+D_t+2(a+s)D_x\right)f(x,y,t)\cdot g(x,y+s,t).}{\tag{39}}
The endpoint pairings correspond to \(\Phi(0)\) and \(\Phi(-h)\).

\begin{prop}
For sufficiently smooth \(f,g\), 
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}\frac{\Phi(0)-\Phi(-h)}h&=\left(D_x^2+D_t+2aD_x\right)f\cdot g_y+2D_xf\cdot g\\&\quad-\frac h2\left[\left(D_x^2+D_t+2aD_x\right)f\cdot g_{yy}+4D_xf\cdot g_y\right]+O(h^2).\end{aligned}}{\tag{40}}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Differentiation gives \(\Phi'(0)=\left(D_x^2+D_t+2aD_x\right)f\cdot g_y+2D_xf\cdot g\) and \(\Phi''(0)=\left(D_x^2+D_t+2aD_x\right)f\cdot g_{yy}+4D_xf\cdot g_y\). Taylor expansion proves (40). For solutions of (5), ordinary differentiation of the first relation gives \(\left(D_x^2+D_t+2aD_x\right)f_y\cdot g+\left(D_x^2+D_t+2aD_x\right)f\cdot g_y=0\), while the second gives \(\left(D_x^2+D_t+2aD_x\right)f_y\cdot g-\left(D_x^2+D_t+2aD_x\right)f\cdot g_y-4D_xf\cdot g=0\). Combining these identities yields \(\Phi'(0)=0\).
\end{proof}


The first-order term in (40) arises from the one-sided placement of the endpoint pairings. A symmetric arrangement is obtained by choosing
\paperdisplay{b=a+\frac h2.}{\tag{41}}
\noindent\textbf{Definition 2.2 (Centered-SD).} Set \(b=a+h/2\). Place \(g_k^{\mathrm C}\) at \(y_k=kh\) and \(f_k^{\mathrm C}\) at \(y_{k-\frac12}=(k-\frac12)h\). The centered semi-discretization, abbreviated Centered-SD, is the bilinear system

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}&\left(D_x^2+D_t+(2a-h)D_x\right)f_k^{\mathrm C}\cdot g_{k-1}^{\mathrm C}=0,\\&\left(D_x^2+D_t+(2a+h)D_x\right)f_k^{\mathrm C}\cdot g_k^{\mathrm C}=0.\end{aligned}}{\tag{42}}
The two neighbouring functions \(g_{k-1}^{\mathrm C},g_k^{\mathrm C}\) are symmetric about \(f_k^{\mathrm C}\), and the two operator parameters are symmetric about \(a\).

Substituting \(b=a+h/2\) into (21) gives
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}\tau_n^{\mathrm C}(k)&=\det_{1\le i,j\le N}\Bigg[c_{ij}+\frac{\alpha_i\beta_j}{p_i+q_j}\left(-\frac{q_j+a}{p_i-a}\right)^n\\&\qquad\qquad\times\left(\frac{1+h/(p_i-a)}{1-h/(q_j+a)}\right)^k\exp\!\big[(p_i+q_j)(x-ht)+(q_j^2-p_i^2)t\big]\Bigg].\end{aligned}}{\tag{43}}
The functions \(f_k^{\mathrm C}=\tau_0^{\mathrm C}(k)\), \(g_k^{\mathrm C}=\tau_1^{\mathrm C}(k)\) satisfy (42) by Theorem 2.1. Both formulations therefore use the same Gram kernel and lattice factor; the change of \(b\) is recorded in their continuous phases.

At fixed \(h\), (42) follows exactly from (18) through (41). For the continuous comparison, \(a\) remains fixed. Using \(\Phi\) from (39) at the location of \(f_k^{\mathrm C}\), the two pairings correspond to \(\Phi(-h/2)\) and \(\Phi(h/2)\).

\begin{prop}
For sufficiently smooth \(f,g\), 
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned} \frac{\Phi(h/2)+\Phi(-h/2)}2&=\left(D_x^2+D_t+2aD_x\right)f\cdot g+O(h^2),\\ \frac{\Phi(h/2)-\Phi(-h/2)}h&=\left(D_x^2+D_t+2aD_x\right)f\cdot g_y+2D_xf\cdot g+O(h^2). \end{aligned}}{\tag{44}}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Taylor expansion gives \(\Phi(\pm h/2)=\Phi(0)\pm(h/2)\Phi'(0)+(h^2/8)\Phi''(0)+O(h^3)\). Taking the sum and difference proves (44). As in the proof of Proposition 2.1, the limiting relations are equivalent to the continuous bilinear pair (5).
\end{proof}


The endpoint and symmetric pairings give first- and second-order bilinear consistency, respectively. To reconstruct smooth real physical fields through logarithmic derivatives, we use a positive parameter class for these determinant solutions.

\begin{prop}
Choose real parameters satisfying \(c_{ij}=\delta_{ij}\), \(\alpha_i<0\), and \(\beta_i>0\). If 
\paperdisplay{-a<q_N<\cdots<q_1<-p_1<\cdots<-p_N,\qquad0<h<q_N+a,}{\tag{45}}
then both determinant families are positive for every integer \(n,k\) and finite real \(x,t\).
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The nonconstant part of each determinant is a product of positive diagonal matrices and the Cauchy matrix with entries \(-1/(p_i+q_j)\). Indeed, \(-(q_j+a)/(p_i-a)>0\) and \((1+h/(p_i-a))/(1-h/(q_j+a))>0\), and these factors separate into row and column factors. Every nonempty principal minor of this Cauchy matrix satisfies 
\paperdisplay{\det_{i,j\in I}\left[-\frac1{p_i+q_j}\right]=\frac{\prod_{i<j,\ i,j\in I}(p_i-p_j)(q_i-q_j)}{\prod_{i,j\in I}(-p_i-q_j)}>0.}{\tag{46}}
Positive diagonal multiplication preserves these signs. Expanding each determinant as the sum of the principal minors of its nonconstant part, including the empty minor \(1\), proves positivity. The additional Centered-SD phase also consists of positive row and column factors.
\end{proof}


The common bilinear lattice and its determinant identity thus support both parameter choices. On the positive class, the resulting determinants yield smooth real physical fields. Their derivative identities are collected in Appendix A.

\section{Integrable nonlinear semi-discrete formulations}

The nonlinear Endpoint-SD system is given by (50)–(51), and the Centered-SD system by (58), closed by the reconstruction (59). Their Darboux–Lax pairs are (65) and (66). We first state these systems in the physical fields and then derive them from the bilinear equations. Section 3.4 examines their continuous limits and the approximation of the exact wave fields.

\subsection{The endpoint-based semi-discretization (Endpoint-SD)}

We reconstruct the endpoint fields from the logarithmic ratio and product of \(f_k^{\mathrm E},g_k^{\mathrm E}\). For a lattice function \(w_k(x,t)\), define

\paperdisplay{\Delta_hw_k=\frac{w_k-w_{k-1}}h.}{\tag{47}}

The Endpoint-SD bilinear equations are

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}&\left(D_x^2+D_t+2aD_x\right)f_k^{\mathrm E}\cdot g_k^{\mathrm E}=0,\\&\left(D_x^2+D_t+(2a-2h)D_x\right)f_k^{\mathrm E}\cdot g_{k-1}^{\mathrm E}=0.\end{aligned}}{\tag{48}}

We define the physical fields by

\paperdisplay{u_k=2\partial_x\log\frac{f_k^{\mathrm E}}{g_k^{\mathrm E}},\qquad v_k=\frac2h\partial_x\log\frac{f_k^{\mathrm E}g_k^{\mathrm E}}{f_{k-1}^{\mathrm E}g_{k-1}^{\mathrm E}}.}{\tag{49}}
The field \(u_k\) is associated with the node \(y_k=kh\), while \(v_k\) lies at the edge midpoint \(y_{k-\frac12}\). These locations are used when comparing the lattice fields with their continuous counterparts.

\begin{samepage}
\begin{prop}
Let \(f_k^{\mathrm E},g_k^{\mathrm E}\) be positive, sufficiently smooth solutions of (48). The physical fields (49) satisfy the Endpoint-SD system

\begin{align*}
\Delta_hu_{k,t}&=-\partial_x\Delta_h\left(\frac12u_k^2+2au_k\right)-v_{k,xx},\tag{50}\\
v_{k,t}&=-\partial_x^2\Delta_hu_k\notag\\
&\quad-\partial_x\Bigg[\left(\frac{u_k+u_{k-1}}2+2a-2h\right)v_k+\frac h4v_k^2\notag\\
&\hspace{24mm}-2(u_k+u_{k-1})-\frac h4(\Delta_hu_k)^2\Bigg].\tag{51}
\end{align*}
\end{prop}
\end{samepage}

\begin{proof}
For either tau family, use the two logarithmic ratios

\paperdisplay{r_k=\log\frac{f_k}{g_k},\qquad q_k=\log\frac{f_k}{g_{k-1}}.}{\tag{52}}
For the common bilinear lattice (18), division by the corresponding tau products gives

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}
r_{k,t}&=r_{k,xx}-r_{k,x}^2-2b r_{k,x}-2\partial_x^2\log f_k,\\
q_{k,t}&=q_{k,xx}-q_{k,x}^2-2(b-h)q_{k,x}-2\partial_x^2\log f_k.
\end{aligned}}{\tag{53}}
Here we used \(\log(f_kg_k)=2\log f_k-r_k\) and \(\log(f_kg_{k-1})=2\log f_k-q_k\). The identities (53) will also be used for the centered formulation and the Lax representation.

For Endpoint-SD, set \(b=a\) and take \((f_k,g_k)=(f_k^{\mathrm E},g_k^{\mathrm E})\). The field definitions imply

\paperdisplay{u_k=2r_{k,x},\qquad q_{k,x}=\frac{u_k+u_{k-1}}4+\frac h4v_k,\qquad
4\Delta_h\partial_x\log f_k=v_k+\Delta_hu_k.}{\tag{54}}
Applying \(2\partial_x\) to the first equation of (53) gives

\paperdisplay{u_{k,t}=u_{k,xx}-\partial_x\left(\frac12u_k^2+2au_k\right)-4\partial_x^3\log f_k.}{\tag{55}}
Taking its lattice difference and using the last identity in (54) yields (50).

For the second field equation, subtract the average of the first relation in (53) and its backward shift from the second relation. Use (54), multiply by \(4/h\), and differentiate in \(x\) to obtain (51). The algebraic identities are given in Appendix B.
\end{proof}

Equations (50)–(51) describe the endpoint fields reconstructed from the bilinear lattice. Applying (49) to the positive Gram determinants of Section 2.3 gives smooth exact fields, which provide reference solutions for the analysis and numerical experiments below.

\subsection{The centered semi-discretization (Centered-SD)}

The centered construction uses the symmetric placement of \(f_k^{\mathrm C}\) between \(g_{k-1}^{\mathrm C}\) and \(g_k^{\mathrm C}\). Its bilinear pairings are

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}&\left(D_x^2+D_t+(2a+h)D_x\right)f_k^{\mathrm C}\cdot g_k^{\mathrm C}=0,\\&\left(D_x^2+D_t+(2a-h)D_x\right)f_k^{\mathrm C}\cdot g_{k-1}^{\mathrm C}=0.\end{aligned}}{\tag{56}}
With \(f_k^{\mathrm C}\) located at \(y_k=(k-1/2)h\), define

\paperdisplay{u_k=\partial_x\log\frac{\left(f_k^{\mathrm C}\right)^2}{g_{k-1}^{\mathrm C}g_k^{\mathrm C}},\qquad
v_k=\partial_x\Delta_h\log(\left(f_k^{\mathrm C}\right)^2g_{k-1}^{\mathrm C}g_k^{\mathrm C}).}{\tag{57}}
The field \(u_k\) is nodal, and \(v_k\) lies at the edge midpoint \(y_k-h/2\). For these exact tau fields, the logarithmic difference is

\[\omega_k=\partial_x\log\frac{g_k^{\mathrm C}}{g_{k-1}^{\mathrm C}}.\]

\begin{samepage}
\begin{prop}
Let \(f_k^{\mathrm C},g_k^{\mathrm C}\) be positive, sufficiently smooth solutions of (56). The physical fields (57) satisfy the Centered-SD system

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned} \Delta_hu_{k,t} &=-\partial_x\Delta_h\left[\frac12u_k^2+2au_k+\frac{\omega_k^2}{2}-h\omega_k\right]-v_{k,xx},\\[2mm] v_{k,t} &=-\partial_x^2\Delta_hu_k\\ &\quad-\partial_x\Bigg\{\Delta_h\left[\frac12u_k^2+2au_k+\frac{\omega_k^2}{2}-h\omega_k\right]\\ &\hspace{22mm}+\frac2h\left[(u_k+2a)\omega_k+(u_{k-1}+2a)\omega_{k-1}-h(u_k+u_{k-1})\right]\Bigg\}. \end{aligned}}{\tag{58}}
Here \(\omega_k\) is reconstructed from the physical fields by

\paperdisplay{\omega_k=\frac h2(v_k-\Delta_hu_k)-\omega_{k-1},\qquad k=1,\ldots,N.}{\tag{59}}
with the compatible lower trace \(\omega_0=\partial_x\log(g_0^{\mathrm C}/g_{-1}^{\mathrm C})\). On a finite lattice, the recurrence uniquely determines \(\omega\) from \(u,v\) and this trace.
\end{prop}
\end{samepage}

Appendix B gives the closed form of the reconstruction and the conditions on the lower trace.

\begin{proof}
Use the ratios (52) with \((f_k,g_k)=(f_k^{\mathrm C},g_k^{\mathrm C})\). Their derivatives satisfy

\paperdisplay{u_k=r_{k,x}+q_{k,x},\qquad
(q_k-r_k)_x=\partial_x\log\frac{g_k}{g_{k-1}}.}{\tag{60}}
The field definitions also give

\paperdisplay{v_k-\Delta_hu_k=\frac2h\partial_x\log\frac{g_k}{g_{k-2}}
=\frac2h\left[\partial_x\log\frac{g_k}{g_{k-1}}+\partial_x\log\frac{g_{k-1}}{g_{k-2}}\right].}{\tag{61}}
By the recurrence (59) and its specified initial value, \(\omega_k=(q_k-r_k)_x=\partial_x\log(g_k/g_{k-1})\). Hence \(r_{k,x}=(u_k-\omega_k)/2\) and \(q_{k,x}=(u_k+\omega_k)/2\). Set \(b=a+h/2\) in (53). Subtracting its first relation from the second and differentiating in \(x\) gives

\paperdisplay{\omega_{k,t}=\omega_{k,xx}-\partial_x[(u_k+2a)\omega_k-hu_k].}{\tag{62}}

Adding the two relations in (53) uses \(r_{k,x}^2+q_{k,x}^2=(u_k^2+\omega_k^2)/2\). Apply \(\partial_x\Delta_h\) to the sum. Since (57) implies \(4\Delta_h\partial_x\log f_k=v_k+\Delta_hu_k\), the second derivatives combine to give the first equation in (58).

Differentiating \(v_k=\Delta_hu_k+2(\omega_k+\omega_{k-1})/h\) in \(t\) and twice in \(x\) gives

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}
v_{k,t}&=\Delta_hu_{k,t}+\frac2h(\omega_{k,t}+\omega_{k-1,t}),\\
v_{k,xx}&=\partial_x^2\Delta_hu_k+\frac2h(\omega_{k,xx}+\omega_{k-1,xx}).
\end{aligned}}{\tag{63}}
Substitution of (62) and the first equation of (58), followed by the second identity in (63), gives the second equation of (58).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Darboux–Lax representations}

Assume that the tau functions are positive, sufficiently smooth solutions of (48) and (56), with the compatible lower trace in (59) for Centered-SD. Let \(Y\) denote the backward shift, \((Y\psi)_k=\psi_{k-1}\). For each tau pair, set
\paperdisplay{S_k=2\partial_x^2\log f_k.}{\tag{64}}

\begin{samepage}
\noindent\textbf{Endpoint-SD.} For the physical fields (49), define
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}
L^{\mathrm E}&=\left(\partial_x+\frac{u_{k-1}}2\right)Y
-\left(\partial_x+\frac{u_k+u_{k-1}+hv_k}{4}-h\right),\\
M^{\mathrm E}&=\partial_x^2-2a\partial_x+S_k.
\end{aligned}}{\tag{65}}
\noindent\textbf{Centered-SD.} For the physical fields (57), define
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}
L^{\mathrm C}&=\left(\partial_x+\frac{u_{k-1}-\omega_{k-1}}2\right)Y
-\left(\partial_x+\frac{u_k+\omega_k}2-h\right),\\
M^{\mathrm C}&=\partial_x^2-(2a+h)\partial_x+S_k.
\end{aligned}}{\tag{66}}
\end{samepage}

Write \([L,M]=LM-ML\).

\begin{prop}
The operators (65)–(66) satisfy
\begin{align*}
L_t^{\mathrm E}+[L^{\mathrm E},M^{\mathrm E}]
&=-\frac12\,\partial_x(u_k+u_{k-1}+hv_k)\,L^{\mathrm E},\tag{67}\\
L_t^{\mathrm C}+[L^{\mathrm C},M^{\mathrm C}]
&=-\partial_x(u_k+\omega_k)\,L^{\mathrm C}.\tag{68}
\end{align*}
\end{prop}

These identities give the compatible linear systems \(L^{\mathrm E}\psi^{\mathrm E}=0\), \(\psi_t^{\mathrm E}=M^{\mathrm E}\psi^{\mathrm E}\) and \(L^{\mathrm C}\psi^{\mathrm C}=0\), \(\psi_t^{\mathrm C}=M^{\mathrm C}\psi^{\mathrm C}\).

\begin{proof}
Use the logarithmic ratios (52), with \(b=a\) for Endpoint-SD and \(b=a+h/2\) for Centered-SD. Set
\paperdisplay{A_k=r_{k-1,x},\qquad C_k=q_{k,x}-h,}{\tag{69}}
so the two operator pairs have the common form
\paperdisplay{L=(\partial_x+A_k)Y-(\partial_x+C_k),\qquad
M=\partial_x^2-2b\partial_x+S_k.}{\tag{70}}
The identity \(q_k-r_{k-1}=\log f_k-\log f_{k-1}\) and the \(x\) derivatives of (53) give
\begin{align*}
S_k-S_{k-1}&=2(C_k-A_k)_x,\tag{71}\\
(A_k)_t&=(A_k)_{xx}-2(b+A_k)(A_k)_x-(S_{k-1})_x,\tag{72}\\
(C_k)_t&=(C_k)_{xx}-2(b+C_k)(C_k)_x-(S_k)_x.\tag{73}
\end{align*}

Expand \(L_t+LM-(M-2C_{k,x})L\) by the product rule and the backward shift of coefficients. The coefficient of \(\partial_xY\) is \(S_{k-1}-S_k+2(C_k-A_k)_x\), which vanishes by (71). After this substitution, the coefficients of \(Y\) and the identity operator vanish by (72) and (73), while the coefficient of \(\partial_x\) cancels identically. Thus
\paperdisplay{L_t+[L,M]=-2C_{k,x}L.}{\tag{74}}
Appendix C records the full operator expansion. For Endpoint-SD, (54) gives \(2C_{k,x}=\tfrac12\partial_x(u_k+u_{k-1}+hv_k)\). For Centered-SD, \(q_{k,x}=(u_k+\omega_k)/2\) gives \(2C_{k,x}=\partial_x(u_k+\omega_k)\). These substitutions prove (67)–(68). Along \(\psi_t=M\psi\), the same identity gives \(\partial_t(L\psi)=(M-2C_{k,x})(L\psi)\), so the lattice constraint is preserved.

The coefficient relations also recover the nonlinear equations. For Endpoint-SD, substituting (54) into (71)–(73) yields
\begin{align*}
u_{k,t}&=u_{k,xx}-(u_k+2a)u_{k,x}-2(S_k)_x,\tag{75}\\
\Delta_hS_k&=\frac12\partial_x(v_k+\Delta_hu_k).\tag{76}
\end{align*}
Taking a lattice difference of the first relation and using the second gives (50). The identity \(h v_{k,t}=4C_{k,t}-u_{k,t}-u_{k-1,t}\), together with (73), gives (51).

For Centered-SD, \(C_k-A_{k+1}=\omega_k-h\). Subtracting the forward shift of (72) from (73) gives the auxiliary evolution (62), while (71) again gives (76). Adding (62) to twice the forward shift of (72), and using \(u_k=2A_{k+1}+\omega_k\), yields
\paperdisplay{u_{k,t}=u_{k,xx}-\partial_x[\frac12u_k^2+2au_k+\omega_k^2/2-h\omega_k]-2(S_k)_x.}{\tag{77}}
Its lattice difference, together with (76), gives the first equation in (58). Differentiating \(v_k=\Delta_hu_k+2(\omega_k+\omega_{k-1})/h\) in time and substituting (62) gives the second equation. Appendix C records the intermediate substitutions for this reconstruction.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Continuous limit and approximation of the physical fields}

We compare the equations at the edge midpoint of each formulation. The physical fields \(u_k,v_k\) occupy their respective node and edge locations, with the centered staggering specified in Section 3.2. For Centered-SD, write

\[W_k=\frac{4\omega_k}{h},\qquad v_k=\Delta_hu_k+\frac{W_k+W_{k-1}}2.\]
The smooth reconstruction assumption below is stated in terms of \(W\).

\Needspace{28\baselineskip}
\begin{samepage}
\begin{prop}
On a fixed compact region, suppose the lattice fields admit smooth reconstructions \(u(x,y,t),v(x,y,t)\), with the derivatives required below bounded uniformly as \(h\to0\).

\noindent\textbf{Endpoint-SD.} For solutions of (50)–(51), the reconstructed fields satisfy

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}
u_{yt}&=-v_{xx}-\partial_x[(u+2a)u_y]+O(h^2),\\[2mm]
v_t&=-u_{xxy}-\partial_x[(u+2a)v-4u]\\
&\quad-\frac h4\partial_x(v^2-u_y^2-8v)+O(h^2).
\end{aligned}}{\tag{78}}
\noindent\textbf{Centered-SD.} For solutions of (58), assume the recurrence (59) selects a smooth reconstructed family with \(W=v-u_y+O(h^2)\), including the derivative bounds required in the equations. Then

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}
u_{yt}&=-v_{xx}-\partial_x[(u+2a)u_y]+O(h^2),\\[2mm]
v_t&=-u_{xxy}-\partial_x[(u+2a)v-4u]+O(h^2).
\end{aligned}}{\tag{79}}
All continuous quantities are evaluated at the corresponding edge midpoint. Under these smoothness and reconstruction assumptions, sampling a solution of the continuous DLW system gives defects of orders \(O(h^2)\) and \(O(h)\) in the two Endpoint-SD equations, and \(O(h^2)\) in both Centered-SD equations.
\end{prop}
\end{samepage}

\begin{proof}
Midpoint expansion gives \(\Delta_hu=u_y+h^2u_{yyy}/24+O(h^4)\) and \(\frac{u(y+h/2)+u(y-h/2)}2=u+h^2u_{yy}/8+O(h^4)\). The first Endpoint-SD equation therefore gives the first relation in (78). In its second equation, the flux becomes 
\paperdisplay{(u+2a)v-4u+u_{xy}+\frac h4(v^2-u_y^2-8v)+O(h^2),}{\tag{80}}
which gives the second relation in (78) after differentiation in \(x\).

For Centered-SD, the reconstruction has the expansion 
\paperdisplay{v=u_y+W+h^2\left(\frac{u_{yyy}}{24}+\frac{W_{yy}}8\right)+O(h^4).}{\tag{81}}
The extra flux in its first equation is \(\omega^2/2-h\omega=h^2(W^2/32-W/4)\). On the stated smooth family, its lattice difference and subsequent \(x\) derivative are \(O(h^2)\), which gives the first relation in (79).

Scaling (62) gives \(W_t=W_{xx}-\partial_x[(u+2a)W-4u]\). Differentiating the reconstruction in time and using this equation, the right-hand side for \(v_t\) is 
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}
&-\partial_y\partial_x\left(\frac12u^2+2au\right)-v_{xx}+(v-u_y)_{xx}
-\partial_x[(u+2a)(v-u_y)-4u]+O(h^2)\\
&=-u_{xxy}-\partial_x[(u+2a)v-4u]+O(h^2).
\end{aligned}}{\tag{82}}
This proves (79).
\end{proof}

The smooth-family assumption includes the lower trace in the reconstruction (59), together with the resulting spatial derivatives. For matched exact waves, this expansion follows from the Gram formulas.

Fix the spectral parameters \(p_i,q_j\) of the continuous family (6)–(7). Their combined phase is \((p_i+q_j)x+(q_j^2-p_i^2)t+[(p_i-a)^{-1}+(q_j+a)^{-1}]y\). To match this family, evaluate the Centered-SD Gram formula (43) at the shifted parameters \(p_i-h/2\) and \(q_j+h/2\), with \(c_{ij}=\delta_{ij}\) and \(\alpha_i\beta_j=-\rho_i((p_i-a)-h/2)/((q_j+a)+h/2)\). The \(x,t\) phase then agrees exactly with the continuous one, and the \(y\) phase rate is
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}\frac1h\log\frac{(p_i-a+h/2)(q_j+a+h/2)}{(p_i-a-h/2)(q_j+a-h/2)}&=\frac1{p_i-a}+\frac1{q_j+a}+O(h^2).\end{aligned}}{\tag{83}}
At their respective staggered positions, the \(g\) amplitude agrees with the continuous amplitude, while the \(f\) amplitude differs by the factor \(\sqrt{(1-h^2/(4(p_i-a)^2))/(1-h^2/(4(q_j+a)^2))}=1+O(h^2)\). For nonzero spectral denominators, sufficiently small \(h\), and positive tau functions bounded away from zero on the fixed region, the determinants and their required derivatives therefore differ from the continuous counterparts by \(O(h^2)\). Symmetric averaging in (57) gives \(u_h=u+O(h^2)\); applying the midpoint difference to the logarithmic potential gives \(v_h=v+O(h^2)\). Thus both exact physical fields approximate their continuous counterparts at second order.

The extra flux also distinguishes the initial derivative errors of the two semi-discrete formulations. With common continuous physical samples and the same lower value of \(u_t\), subtracting their first equations and summing along the lattice gives 
\paperdisplay{(u_{k,t})_{\mathrm C}-(u_{k,t})_{\mathrm E} =-\partial_x\left[\left(\frac{\omega_k^2}{2}-h\omega_k\right)-\left(\frac{\omega_0^2}{2}-h\omega_0\right)\right].}{\tag{84}}
On the smooth reconstructed family, this difference is \(O(h^2)\). The two formulations therefore share the second-order approximation of the first equation while retaining different leading derivative errors. This identity connects the nonlinear flux to the initial spatial comparisons in Section 5.2. The midpoint expansions behind (78) and (79) are given in Appendix C.

\section{Numerical formulation}

In this section, we develop numerical formulations for computing the physical fields introduced above. The semi-discrete equations retain continuous dependence on \(x\) and \(t\), so their numerical evolution requires a further spatial approximation, initial and boundary data, and a time-integration scheme. We specify these ingredients for Endpoint-SD and Centered-SD, together with a direct finite-difference discretization of the continuous DLW system as a baseline. Section 4.1 introduces the common differentiation operators in \(x\) and the resulting spatial formulations. Section 4.2 specifies the initial and boundary data and describes how the fields are advanced in time. In Section 4.3, we use the Centered-SD conservation law to construct a shared moving mesh, allowing the longitudinal resolution to adapt to the evolving fields.


\subsection{Fourier differentiation and spatial formulations}

We first discretize the remaining \(x\) derivatives to obtain systems that can be advanced in time. Endpoint-SD, Centered-SD, and the direct finite-difference baseline use the same Fourier differentiation in \(x\), providing a common longitudinal approximation for examining their different transverse formulations. Write the periodic interval as \([-L,L)\), where \(L\) is its half-width and the period is \(2L\). On Fourier mode \(m\), the numerical operators \(\widetilde{\partial}_x\) and \(\widetilde{\partial}_{xx}\) have multipliers \(\mathrm i\pi m/L\) and \(-(\pi m/L)^2\), respectively. The first-derivative multiplier is set to zero at the Nyquist frequency, and nonlinear products are evaluated at the grid points. Throughout the numerical formulation, the tilde denotes the numerical derivative in the physical coordinate x. This subsection uses Fourier differentiation. Section 4.3 specifies the mapped three-point realization used for both meshes in the fixed-versus-adaptive comparison.

Both semi-discrete formulations place \(u\) at nodes and \(v\) at edge midpoints; FD places both fields at nodes. We use the backward transverse difference (47) and write adjacent averages and centered transverse differences explicitly. Recalling the physical-field equations (50)–(51), Endpoint-SD takes the form

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}\Delta_h\dot u_k&=-\widetilde{\partial}_x\Delta_h\left(\frac12u_k^2+2au_k\right)-\widetilde{\partial}_{xx}v_k,\\\dot v_k&=-\widetilde{\partial}_x\Bigg[\left(\frac{u_k+u_{k-1}}2+2a-2h\right)v_k+\frac h4v_k^2\\&\hspace{24mm}-2(u_k+u_{k-1})-\frac h4(\Delta_hu_k)^2\Bigg]-\widetilde{\partial}_{xx}\Delta_hu_k.\end{aligned}}{\tag{85}}

For Centered-SD, the field definition in Section 3.2 gives \(v_k=\Delta_hu_k+2(\omega_k+\omega_{k-1})/h\). We retain this relation in the numerical formulation. The equations for the time derivatives are

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}\Delta_h\dot u_k&=-\widetilde{\partial}_x\Delta_h[\frac12u_k^2+2au_k+\omega_k^2/2-h\omega_k]-\widetilde{\partial}_{xx}v_k,\\\dot\omega_k&=\widetilde{\partial}_{xx}\omega_k-\widetilde{\partial}_x[(u_k+2a)\omega_k-hu_k],\\\dot v_k&=\Delta_h\dot u_k+\frac2h(\dot\omega_k+\dot\omega_{k-1}).\end{aligned}}{\tag{86}}
The first equation is summed from the prescribed lower value of \(\dot u\). Advancing \((u,\omega)\) and recovering \(v\) through the field relation implements the physical-field equations of Proposition 3.2. The same differentiated relation supplies \(\dot v\) when measuring the initial spatial error.

For comparison, direct discretization of the continuous DLW system gives the FD baseline,

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}\Delta_h\dot u_k&=-\widetilde{\partial}_x\Delta_h\left(\frac12u_k^2+2au_k\right)-\widetilde{\partial}_{xx}\frac{v_k+v_{k-1}}2,\\\dot v_k&=-\widetilde{\partial}_x[(u_k+2a)v_k-4u_k]-\widetilde{\partial}_{xx}\frac{u_{k+1}-u_{k-1}}{2h}.\end{aligned}}{\tag{87}}

The three spatial formulations use the same Fourier differentiation in \(x\) and the prescribed lower-boundary data for recovering the \(u\) time derivative. Their different approximations enter through the transverse differences, nonlinear fluxes, and placement of the physical fields. This common numerical setting allows us to examine how the endpoint and centered lattice constructions affect the computed waves, with direct finite differences providing a reference discretization of the continuous equations. It also connects the consistency analysis of Section 3 with the spatial errors measured in the numerical experiments.


\subsection{Initial and boundary data and time integration}

In this subsection, we specify the initial and boundary data and describe how the resulting spatial systems are advanced in time. The initial states are obtained by sampling the same continuous reference fields at the native locations of each formulation. The state advanced in time is

\[\boldsymbol z=\begin{cases}(u_k,v_k)_k,&\text{Endpoint-SD and FD},\\(u_k,\omega_k)_k,&\text{Centered-SD}.\end{cases}\]
Each component includes its values at all longitudinal nodes. Centered-SD reconstructs \(v\) through the relation in Section 4.1. At each stage, the current state and the prescribed boundary data supply the time derivatives required by the integrator. We use forward Euler, classical RK4, and Crank–Nicolson to examine how different temporal approximations affect the computed physical fields.

For Centered-SD, the initial lower auxiliary value is \(\omega_0=h(v^*-u_y^*)/4\), evaluated at the lower node. The remaining values follow from the linear reconstruction (59),

\paperdisplay{\omega_k=\frac h2(v_k-\Delta_hu_k)-\omega_{k-1}.}{\tag{88}}
This recurrence reproduces the prescribed physical field \(v\) on every internal edge. During evolution, the lower auxiliary value follows the same \(\omega\) equation as the interior values. The lower value of \(u_t\) is supplied by the continuous reference solution at every stage.

Endpoint-SD uses the prescribed lower derivative and the continuous lower ghost value of \(u\) in its backward lattice relations. For FD, the lower data are supplied in the same way, while the upper ghost value extrapolates the departure from the continuous reference. Writing \(e_k=u_k-u_k^*\), this closure is \(u_{N+1}=u_{N+1}^*+3e_N-3e_{N-1}+e_{N-2}\). The reference values are evaluated at the current stage time. Fourier differentiation acts on a periodic \(x\) interval extending beyond the principal wave profiles; the products are evaluated at the nodes without filtering, dealiasing or artificial viscosity.

Let \(t_n=n\Delta t\), and let \(\boldsymbol z^n\) denote the state at \(t_n\). Writing the evolution equations as \(\dot{\boldsymbol z}=F(t,\boldsymbol z)\), forward Euler advances the state by \(\boldsymbol z^{n+1}=\boldsymbol z^n+\Delta t F(t_n,\boldsymbol z^n)\). Classical RK4 uses four stage derivatives,

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}K_1&=F(t_n,\boldsymbol z^n),&K_2&=F(t_n+\Delta t/2,\boldsymbol z^n+\Delta t K_1/2),\\K_3&=F(t_n+\Delta t/2,\boldsymbol z^n+\Delta t K_2/2),&K_4&=F(t_n+\Delta t,\boldsymbol z^n+\Delta t K_3),\\\boldsymbol z^{n+1}&=\boldsymbol z^n+\frac{\Delta t}{6}(K_1+2K_2+2K_3+K_4).\end{aligned}}{\tag{89}}

Crank–Nicolson determines the next state from

\paperdisplay{\boldsymbol z^{n+1}=\boldsymbol z^n+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\bigl[F(t_n,\boldsymbol z^n)+F(t_{n+1},\boldsymbol z^{n+1})\bigr].}{\tag{90}}
We solve the implicit step by fixed-point iteration from an Euler prediction, using the scaled state \((u,W)\), where \(W=4\omega/h\). Appendix D gives the residual tolerance and the tighter-tolerance control calculation.

The spatial and temporal comparisons in Sections 5.2 and 5.3 use \(\Delta t=10^{-4}\) and \(T=0.01\); the mesh experiments in Section 5.4 use RK4 with the same step size and final time. In each comparison, the advanced state is expressed in terms of the physical fields \(u\) and \(v\) for error evaluation. This provides a common basis for assessing the numerical evolution across formulations with different state variables. With the spatial operators and boundary treatment fixed, the time-integrator comparisons then allow us to follow how the temporal approximation influences the accumulation of errors in the two coupled fields.


\subsection{A conservation-based moving mesh}

The spatial variation of a wave is often concentrated in a small part of the computational domain. A uniform grid distributes nodes equally across these regions and the more slowly varying background, while an adaptive mesh can allocate additional resolution where the fields vary more strongly. In this subsection, we first establish a semi-discrete conservation law for Centered-SD and then use its density and flux to construct a self-adaptive moving mesh. The nodes move in \(x\), while the \(y\) lattice remains uniform and all transverse layers share the same longitudinal mesh. This arrangement retains the transverse lattice pairings and allows the distribution of resolution in \(x\) to evolve with the physical fields.

To establish the conservation law, we introduce the density \(\rho_k=1-\omega_k/h\) and define the associated flux \(q_k\) by

\paperdisplay{\rho_k=1-\frac{\omega_k}{h},\qquad q_k=(u_k+2a)\rho_k-\partial_x\rho_k-2a.}{\tag{91}}
Substituting \(\omega_k=h(1-\rho_k)\) into the auxiliary equation of Centered-SD gives \(\partial_t\rho_k+\partial_xq_k=0\). We choose the additive constant in \(q_k\) so that the flux vanishes on the zero-field background. This conservation relation connects the evolution of the density to its spatial transport and forms the basis of the mesh construction below.

Since all layers share the same \(x\) nodes, we average the density and the flux over the stored layers,

\paperdisplay{\bar\rho=\frac1{N_y}\sum_k\rho_k,\qquad \bar q=\frac1{N_y}\sum_k q_k,}{\tag{92}}
and the averaged quantities satisfy the same conservation relation.

On a periodic interval of length \(2L\), the initial nodes equidistribute the cumulative density. Fixing a reference node \(x_0\), we require

\paperdisplay{\int_{x_0}^{x_i(0)}\bar\rho\,dx=\frac{i}{N_x}\int_{x_0}^{x_0+2L}\bar\rho\,dx,\qquad i=0,\dots,N_x,}{\tag{93}}
so intervals in regions of larger \(\bar\rho\) receive more nodes.

During the evolution each node keeps its cumulative-density label, which is equivalent to preserving the mass of every cell \([x_i,x_{i+1}]\). This requires \(\bar\rho_i\mathcal V_i-\bar q_i\) to take the same value at every node. We choose that constant as the negative of the nodal mean of \(\bar q\), and the velocity is

\paperdisplay{\dot x_i=\mathcal V_i=\frac{\bar q_i-\langle\bar q\rangle}{\bar\rho_i},\qquad \langle\bar q\rangle=\frac1{N_x}\sum_i\bar q_i.}{\tag{94}}
This choice gives zero density-weighted mean nodal velocity and keeps the mapping periodic. The construction requires \(\bar\rho>0\), and the ordering of the nodes is preserved while the discrete Jacobian remains positive.

For the mesh comparison, both the fixed and adaptive calculations use mapped three-point differences in the longitudinal direction. Write \(\xi_i=\xi_0+i\Delta\xi\) for the fixed uniform computational grid and \(x_i=\xi_i+s_i\) for the physical nodes. Discretizing the chain rule gives

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}&(\widetilde{\partial}_x f)_i=\frac{f_{i+1}-f_{i-1}}{2\Delta\xi\,J_i},\\&(\widetilde{\partial}_{xx}f)_i=\frac{f_{i+1}-2f_i+f_{i-1}}{(\Delta\xi)^2J_i^2}\\&\quad-\frac{(J_{i+1}-J_{i-1})(f_{i+1}-f_{i-1})}{4(\Delta\xi)^2J_i^3},\\&J_i=1+\frac{s_{i+1}-s_{i-1}}{2\Delta\xi}.\end{aligned}}{\tag{95}}
Here \(J_i\) is the discrete Jacobian, and \(f_i\) denotes a sampled field. The formulas use centered three-point differences on the computational grid. Setting \(s_i=0\) gives \(J_i=1\) and the uniform-grid realization used in Section 5.4.

Here \(i\) labels the moving longitudinal nodes and \(k\) labels the transverse layers. All layers share \(x_i(t)\) and its velocity \(\mathcal V_i\). A dot denotes the total time derivative along a moving node, so we advance

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}\dot u_{i,k}&=F_{u,i,k}+\mathcal V_i(\widetilde{\partial}_xu)_{i,k},\\\dot\omega_{i,k}&=F_{\omega,i,k}+\mathcal V_i(\widetilde{\partial}_x\omega)_{i,k},\qquad\dot x_i=\mathcal V_i.\end{aligned}}{\tag{96}}
Here \(F_{u,i,k}\) and \(F_{\omega,i,k}\) are the Centered-SD rates at fixed physical position, evaluated with the current mapped operators. The common node velocity retains the reconstruction of \(v\) across layers. At the lower transverse boundary \(k=0\), each longitudinal node uses

\[\dot u_{i,0}=u_t^*(x_i(t),y_0,t)+\mathcal V_i u_x^*(x_i(t),y_0,t).\]
Setting \(\mathcal V_i=0\) gives the fixed-grid equations.

The resulting scheme couples the physical-field evolution to a mesh determined by the semi-discrete conservation law. The averaged density specifies the initial distribution of nodes, and the associated flux governs their subsequent motion through the preservation of cumulative-density labels. By sharing the longitudinal nodes across all transverse layers, the construction accommodates mesh adaptation within the existing Centered-SD field reconstruction. It thus provides a concrete way to use the analytical structure of the equations to guide spatial resolution, whose effect on the computed wave fields can be assessed at a fixed node count.

\subsubsection*{One-soliton and two-soliton cases}

Figure 5(a,c) shows the one-soliton profiles along \(y=0\). Both fields form a localized depression, with a single trough and a return toward the background on either side. Read together with the spatial comparison, these results show why the initial derivative accuracy, the terminal field error and the response to a smaller time step describe different aspects of the same computation.

\subsection{Fixed and adaptive meshes}

In this subsection, we use the semi-discrete conservation law to examine how the distribution of spatial resolution affects the accuracy of Centered-SD. The soliton fields concentrate their variation along localized wave bands, and the conservation density provides a means of placing additional nodes in these regions. We compare uniform and adaptive meshes for the one- and two-soliton problems, following both the node distribution and the resulting physical-field errors. This allows us to examine how a conserved quantity from the semi-discrete system can guide the numerical resolution of its waves.

Both calculations use the same number of shared \(x\) nodes, the same uniform transverse lattice with \(h=0.1\), and RK4 with \(\Delta t=10^{-4}\) up to \(T=0.01\). The uniform \(x\) spacing is \(\Delta x=0.1\). On both meshes, the \(x\) derivatives are evaluated using the mapped three-point centered difference operators of Section 4.3, so that the comparison uses a common spatial approximation while varying the node distribution. The soliton backgrounds provide a positive transverse-averaged conservation density for the equidistribution construction.

In the adaptive runs, the initial \(x\) nodes are placed by equidistributing the transverse-averaged density \(\bar\rho\) computed from the exact initial fields, and the nodes subsequently evolve according to the conservation-based velocity of Section 4.3. The averaged density ranges from \(1.000\) in the background to \(1.150\) across the wave core in the one-soliton case, and from \(1.000\) to \(1.050\) in the wider domain of the two-soliton case. Figure 11 plots the resulting local grid spacing, normalized by the uniform spacing \(\Delta x=0.1\), at \(t=0\) and \(T=0.01\).

As seen in Figure 11, the equidistribution concentrates nodes in the region of larger density. In the one-soliton test, the local spacing contracts to \(0.90\Delta x\) across the wave trough while stretching to \(1.04\Delta x\) in the background; in the two-soliton test, it varies more mildly between \(0.97\Delta x\) and \(1.02\Delta x\). Over the short evolution to \(T=0.01\), the initial and final node distributions are nearly indistinguishable: the maximum node displacement is \(9.5\times10^{-4}\) in the one-soliton case and \(5.2\times10^{-4}\) in the two-soliton case, and the total discrete mass of \(\bar\rho\) is conserved to within \(10^{-7}\) relative error. The spatial resolution pattern is thus established by the initial equidistribution and adjusted only slightly by the node motion during the run.

\subsection{Evolution of independent physical-field perturbations}

The preceding experiments start from prescribed reference wave fields and examine how accurately their evolution is reproduced. A related question concerns the response of the numerical evolution when the initial physical fields are slightly changed. Such changes may arise when initial data are sampled or prescribed, and their influence develops through the coupling between the two fields. A disturbance introduced into \(u\) can generate a response in \(v\), while a disturbance in \(v\) can also affect the subsequent evolution of \(u\). Understanding this response helps describe how the computed waves depend on their initial physical data.

We examine this dependence on the same four backgrounds by perturbing \(u_0\) and \(v_0\) separately. For each spatial formulation, the perturbed evolution is measured relative to its own unperturbed trajectory. This separates the continuing response in the initially disturbed field from the response generated in the other component, allowing us to follow both processes over time. Let \(y_L,y_R\) be the endpoints of the transverse node interval for the chosen test. The two experiments add \(\varepsilon\Phi\) to \(u_0\) or \(v_0\), respectively, with

\paperdisplay{\Phi(x,y)=\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\pi(y-y_L)}{y_R-y_L}\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi x}{2}\right),\qquad\varepsilon=10^{-6}.}{\tag{101}}
The perturbation is sampled at each field's native sites. The transverse node intervals are \([-9.95,9.95]\) for the one-soliton case, \([-39.95,39.95]\) for the two-soliton case, and \([-2,2]\) for the Gaussian and sinusoidal cases. The envelope and its first derivative vanish at the transverse endpoints. All trajectories use \(h=\Delta x=0.1\), RK4, \(\Delta t=10^{-4}\), and \(T=0.01\), with the prescribed lower \(u\) trace and the boundary closures of Section 4.2.

For each spatial formulation, let \((u_h^\varepsilon,v_h^\varepsilon)\) and \((u_h^0,v_h^0)\) be its perturbed and unperturbed numerical trajectories. We record \(\delta u_h=u_h^\varepsilon-u_h^0\) and \(\delta v_h=v_h^\varepsilon-v_h^0\), and plot their native-node maximum magnitudes divided by \(\varepsilon\) in the observation regions of Section 5.1. The initial ordinates are the maxima of the imposed perturbation sampled at those same sites. The nonlinear difference equations are advanced together with the corresponding background at every RK4 stage.

For the one-soliton background, Figure 13 follows both perturbation directions. \section{Conclusion and discussion}

We have developed two related integrable semi-discretizations of the \((2+1)\)-dimensional DLW system within a common bilinear framework. Starting from the Hirota–Miwa equation, the construction yields endpoint and centered formulations with explicit Gram determinant solutions, a common positive parameter class, and compatible Darboux–Lax representations. Their physical-field equations make the consequences of lattice placement and reconstruction explicit. Endpoint-SD is second-order consistent in the first equation and first-order consistent in the second, whereas Centered-SD is second-order consistent in both on the specified smooth reconstructed branches. Matching the centered Gram solutions to the continuous family further establishes second-order approximation of both exact physical fields under the stated regularity and positivity conditions.

The numerical experiments examine how these analytical properties are reflected in the computed waves. Endpoint-SD gives the smallest initial \(u_t\) error in all four tests and retains its advantage in the terminal \(u\) error on the Gaussian and sinusoidal backgrounds, while Centered-SD gives smaller terminal errors in both fields in the soliton tests. Time-step controls support component-dependent cancellation and reinforcement of spatial and temporal errors. The conservation-based mesh improves both field errors at a fixed node count in the soliton tests, with most of the short-time gain arising from the initial node distribution. Independent initial perturbations reveal the responses generated through coupling between the fields. Together, these experiments show how wave profile, field component, and numerical realization affect the accuracy and response of the discrete evolution.

Taken together, the analytical and numerical results clarify the connection between integrable lattice structure and physical-wave approximation. The shared determinant and Lax constructions allow the two formulations to be compared within a common setting, while their reconstructed equations expose differences in consistency and coupling. Exact solutions then connect the continuum analysis to measurable field errors. This comparison shows why the assessment of a lattice benefits from following its consequences through several stages, from the bilinear pairings and physical-field reconstruction to the approximation of exact waves and their numerical evolution. The observed dependence on background and component also gives context to the formal consistency orders.

Beyond the DLW system, this study suggests a way to organize the analysis and computation of integrable wave equations around the physical fields they describe. Related discrete constructions can be examined through their reconstructed equations, matched exact solutions, and componentwise numerical errors, while available conservation laws can inform the allocation of resolution. Applying this approach to other multi-component systems will require establishing the relevant reconstruction, regularity, and continuum matching conditions for each model. Such studies could help identify which features of an integrable construction determine approximation quality and how those features can guide the design of numerical methods for nonlinear waves.

\section*{Appendix A. Determinant identities and alternative solution representations}

Every field used in the main text is a logarithmic derivative of a \(\tau\) function, so its evaluation reduces to an identity for a determinant and for the derivatives of that determinant. This appendix records the three identities on which the reconstruction rests, together with the relation between the two determinant families and the explicit low-order members.

\noindent\textbf{Rank-one identity.} Let \(M(\theta)=M_0+\theta\,r\,c^{\mathsf T}\) with \(M_0\) invertible and \(r,c\) column vectors. Factoring \(M_0\) out of the rows of \(M(\theta)\) gives

\paperdisplay{\det M(\theta)=\det M_0\left(1+\theta\,c^{\mathsf T}M_0^{-1}r\right),\qquad \partial_\theta\log\det M(\theta)=c^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}r .}{\tag{A1}}
The second form follows from the first together with the Sherman–Morrison formula for \(M^{-1}\), and it is the only identity needed to differentiate a determinant in a parameter that enters linearly.

\noindent\textbf{Shift identity.} The two families (38) and (43) differ only in their phases. The endpoint exponent is \((p_i+q_j)x+(q_j^2-p_i^2)t\), and the centered exponent includes the additional term \(-h(p_i+q_j)t\). The endpoint family gives the centered one through the simultaneous substitutions

\paperdisplay{\tau_n^{\mathrm C}(k;h)=\left.\tau_n^{\mathrm E}(k;h)\right|_{\substack{a\mapsto a+h/2\\p_i\mapsto p_i+h/2,\ q_j\mapsto q_j-h/2}},}{\tag{A2}}
These substitutions preserve \(p_i-a\), \(q_j+a\) and \(p_i+q_j\), so the layer factors and Cauchy minors agree. Their effect on the endpoint phase gives precisely the centered phase in (43), retaining the corresponding positivity conditions.

\noindent\textbf{Logarithmic fields.} With \(f_k=\tau_0(k)\) and \(g_k=\tau_1(k)\) the definitions (49) and (57) become ratios of neighbouring determinants,

\paperdisplay{u_k=2\partial_x\log\frac{\tau_0(k)}{\tau_1(k)},\qquad v_k=\frac2h\partial_x\log\frac{\tau_0(k)\tau_1(k)}{\tau_0(k-1)\tau_1(k-1)},}{\tag{A3}}
and analogously for \(u_k,v_k\) through (57). Positivity of sufficiently smooth \(\tau\) functions is a sufficient condition for smooth real physical-field reconstruction. Proposition 2.3 provides this condition for the parameter class (45). On any compact region in \((x,t)\), with a fixed finite set of lattice indices, these determinants have a positive lower bound and their derivatives are bounded. The logarithmic derivatives defining the fields and their required derivatives are therefore bounded there. More generally, nonvanishing real \(\tau\) functions permit the same local derivative formulas with \(\log|\tau|\); field smoothness alone does not require a positive choice of \(\tau\).

\noindent\textbf{Low-order members.} For \(N=1\) and \(c_{11}=1\), the common family takes the form
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}\tau_n^{(b)}(k)&=1+\frac{\alpha\beta}{p+q}\left(-\frac{q+a}{p-a}\right)^n\\&\quad\times\left(\frac{1+h/(p-a)}{1-h/(q+a)}\right)^k\exp\![(p+q)(x-2(b-a)t)+(q^2-p^2)t].\end{aligned}}{\tag{A4}}
Here \(b=a\) for Endpoint-SD and \(b=a+h/2\) for Centered-SD. Substitution into (A3) reproduces the one-soliton field at its node and edge locations. The two-soliton case in Section 5 uses the corresponding \(2\times2\) determinant in (21). The family with \(c_{ij}=\delta_{ij}\) is the Gram reduction of the Casoratian determinants of the hierarchy [15].


\section*{Appendix B. Field transformations and reconstruction conditions}

\noindent\textbf{Endpoint field equation.} Use the logarithmic ratios (52) and identities (53)–(54). For the second field equation, subtract the average of the first equation in (53) and its backward shift from the second. The required identities are

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}
q_k-\frac{r_k+r_{k-1}}2&=\frac h2\Delta_h\log(f_kg_k),\\
q_{k,x}^2-\frac{u_k^2+u_{k-1}^2}{8}
&=\frac h8(u_k+u_{k-1})v_k+\frac{h^2}{16}\left[v_k^2-(\Delta_hu_k)^2\right].
\end{aligned}}{\tag{B1}}
Using (54) to combine the second derivatives, and multiplying the difference by \(4/h\), we obtain

\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}
0={}&2\partial_t\Delta_h\log(f_kg_k)+(\Delta_hu_k)_x
+(\frac{u_k+u_{k-1}}2+2a-2h)v_k\\
&+\frac h4v_k^2-2(u_k+u_{k-1})-\frac h4(\Delta_hu_k)^2.
\end{aligned}}{\tag{B2}}
Differentiating in \(x\) and using \(v_k=2\partial_x\Delta_h\log(f_kg_k)\) proves (51).

\noindent\textbf{Centered field reconstruction.}

The numerical state of Centered-SD is the pair \((u_k,\omega_k)\), whereas the physical comparison is made in the fields \((u,v)\). This appendix records the two transformations between them, the conditions under which they are equivalent, and the lower trace required by the computation.

\noindent\textbf{Field relation and recurrence.} The definitions (57) imply, for every edge,

\paperdisplay{v_k-\Delta_hu_k=\frac2h\left(\omega_k+\omega_{k-1}\right),\qquad \omega_k=\partial_x\log\frac{g_k}{g_{k-1}},}{\tag{B3}}
where the second expression shows that \(\omega_k\) is the lattice difference of the logarithmic potential and therefore carries a factor \(h\) relative to \(v-u_y\). Rearranging (B3) gives the reconstruction (59),

\paperdisplay{\omega_k=\frac h2\left(v_k-\Delta_hu_k\right)-\omega_{k-1},\qquad k=1,\dots,N .}{\tag{B4}}
The recurrence is linear and lower triangular, so it determines \((\omega_1,\dots,\omega_N)\) uniquely from the physical fields and the single lower value \(\omega_0\). Its solution is

\paperdisplay{\omega_k=(-1)^k \omega_0+\frac h2\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\left(v_j-\Delta_hu_j\right),}{\tag{B5}}
in which the coefficients have modulus one. An error in the lower trace alone propagates with alternating sign and unchanged magnitude, while errors in the edge differences can accumulate through the sum. The reconstruction is an exact affine relation and requires no branch selection.

\noindent\textbf{Conditions on the lower trace.} For the \(\tau\) functions of Theorem 2.1 the compatible trace is fixed by the lattice, namely \(\omega_0=\partial_x\log(g_0^{\mathrm C}/g_{-1}^{\mathrm C})\). In a computation the data are prescribed in the physical fields, so the trace is taken instead from the continuous reference, \(\omega_0=h(v^*-u_y^*)/4\) evaluated at the lower node. The two agree to \(O(h^2)\) on the smooth class of Section 3.4, and the resulting discrepancy enters (B5) with alternating sign only, that is without growth along the layer index. During the evolution the lower value is advanced by the same equation as the interior, so the trace is not frozen.

\noindent\textbf{Consistency of the initial data.} Equation (B4) reproduces \(v_k\) on every edge by construction. Once the lower trace is supplied, \((u,\omega)\) represents the prescribed physical fields \((u,v)\). Changing the lower trace by \(\delta \omega_0\) and repeating the recurrence adds \(\delta\omega_k=(-1)^k\delta \omega_0\). Its adjacent values sum to zero, so the reconstructed initial \(v_k\) remains unchanged. This alternating auxiliary component can affect the subsequent evolution; the lower trace is therefore specified together with the physical initial data. All computations in Section 5 use the construction above.


\section*{Appendix C. Operator compatibility and continuum expansions}

We record the operator expansion used in the proof of Proposition 3.3 and the remaining field-reconstruction calculations. The continuum expansions used in Section 3.4 follow below.

\noindent\textbf{Operator expansion.} With the coefficients and operators (69)–(70), the product rule gives
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}
L_t+LM-(M-2C_{k,x})L
={}&\big[S_{k-1}-S_k+2(C_k-A_k)_x\big]\partial_xY\\
&+\Big[A_{k,t}-A_{k,xx}+2bA_{k,x}+(S_{k-1})_x\\
&\qquad+A_k(S_{k-1}-S_k)+2A_kC_{k,x}\Big]Y\\
&-C_{k,t}+C_{k,xx}-2(b+C_k)C_{k,x}-S_{k,x}.
\end{aligned}}{\tag{C1}}
The first bracket vanishes by (71). Substitution of \(S_{k-1}-S_k=-2(C_k-A_k)_x\) into the second bracket reduces it to \(A_{k,t}-A_{k,xx}+2(b+A_k)A_{k,x}+(S_{k-1})_x\), which vanishes by (72). The last line vanishes by (73), proving (74).

\noindent\textbf{Recovery of the nonlinear systems.} For Endpoint-SD, the first field equation follows from (75)–(76), as shown in the main text. The second follows by substituting (73) and (75) into
\paperdisplay{h(v_k)_t=4(C_k)_t-(u_k)_t-(u_{k-1})_t.}{\tag{C2}}
For Centered-SD, the coefficients obey
\paperdisplay{C_k-A_{k+1}=\omega_k-h.}{\tag{C3}}
To eliminate the second derivatives, use \(-v_{k,xx}+2(\omega_{k,xx}+\omega_{k-1,xx})/h=-\partial_x^2\Delta_hu_k\). \noindent\textbf{Midpoint expansions.} For smooth \(q\) sampled at the nodes and edges of the lattice,

\paperdisplay{\Delta_hq_k=q_y+\frac{h^2}{24}q_{yyy}+O(h^4),\qquad \frac{q_k+q_{k-1}}2=q+\frac{h^2}{8}q_{yy}+O(h^4),}{\tag{C4}}
evaluated at the edge midpoint. Substituting (C4) into the first Endpoint-SD equation gives the first relation in (78), since both the difference and the average are second-order accurate there. The second equation carries an additional term from the flux. Expanding its bracket at the midpoint gives

\paperdisplay{(u+2a)v-4u+u_{xy}+\frac h4\left(v^2-u_y^2-8v\right)+O(h^2),}{\tag{C5}}
and applying \(\partial_x\) and solving for \(v_t\) gives the correction \(-\tfrac h4\partial_x(v^2-u_y^2-8v)\) in (78).

\noindent\textbf{Centered reconstruction.} The relation \(v_k=\Delta_hu_k+\frac{W_k+W_{k-1}}2\) with \(W_k=4\omega_k/h\) expands as

\paperdisplay{v=u_y+W+h^2\left(\frac{u_{yyy}}{24}+\frac{W_{yy}}8\right)+O(h^4),}{\tag{C6}}
so on the smooth family with \(W=v-u_y+O(h^2)\) the reconstructed second field is second-order accurate. The extra flux of the first equation is \(\omega^2/2-h\omega=h^2(W^2/32-W/4)\), whose lattice difference and subsequent \(x\) derivative are \(O(h^2)\), which gives the first relation in (79). Differentiating (C6) in time and using \(W_t=W_{xx}-\partial_x[(u+2a)W-4u]\) from (62) gives the second relation in (79).


\section*{Appendix D. Numerical implementation and supplementary experiments}

This appendix collects the conventions of the computations, the closures at the transverse ends, and the control runs quoted in Sections 5.3 and 5.4.

\noindent\textbf{\(x\) derivatives.} The fixed-grid spatial and time-integrator comparisons use periodic Fourier operators on \([-L,L)\). \section*{Appendix E. Non-soliton reference solutions}
  

We construct the two non-soliton benchmarks by choosing a transverse parameter in a heat-equation representation of the DLW system. The construction uses a Laplace transformation of the associated Boiti–Leon–Pempinelli system, as studied in [16]. The parameter choice below produces the initial profiles of Section 5.1 and gives their subsequent continuous evolution.

 

\noindent\textbf{Linear evolutions.} Write \(X=x-4t\), \(s(y)=A\sin[\pi(y+2)/4]/2\), and denote the prescribed longitudinal profile by \(F_0\). Let \(C\) be its mean and choose \(J_0(X)=CX+J_{\rm per}(X)\), with \(J_0'=F_0\) and zero-mean periodic \(J_{\rm per}\). Here \(C=0\) for the cosine and \(C=\sqrt\pi/32\) for the periodic Gaussian. Treating \(s\) as an independent parameter, solve

 
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}F_t&=-F_{XX},&F(X,0)&=F_0(X),\\ H_t&=H_{XX},&H(X,s,0)&=-s e^{-sJ_0(X)}.\end{aligned}}{\tag{E1}}
The chosen profiles give explicit backward-heat evolutions,

 
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}F_{\rm S}(X,t)&=e^{(\pi/12)^2t}\cos(\pi X/12),\\ F_{\rm G}(X,t)&=\frac{3}{\sqrt{9-t}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(X+192j)^2}{4(9-t)}\right],\quad t<9.\end{aligned}}{\tag{E2}}

The forward-heat solution \(H\) is obtained from the Fourier series of \(e^{-sJ_{\rm per}}\), retaining the factor \(e^{-sCX}\). Define \(\psi\) by

 
\paperdisplay{\psi_t=FH_X-F_XH,\qquad\psi(X,s,0)=e^{-sJ_0(X)}.}{\tag{E3}}
Equation (E1) gives \(\partial_t(FH)=\partial_X(FH_X-F_XH)\). Since \(\psi_X=FH\) at \(t=0\), this identity holds throughout the evolution and fixes the spatial derivative needed in the reconstruction.

 

\noindent\textbf{Physical fields and initial matching.} Define the denominator

\[D=\psi H_s-H\psi_s.\]
In the following formulas, \(s\) is treated as an independent parameter when differentiating. In particular, \(u_s\) differentiates the first expression before substituting \(s=s(y)\). The transformed fields are

 
\paperdisplay{\begin{aligned}u(x,y,t)&=-2\left(\frac{D_X}{D}-\frac{\psi_X}{\psi}\right),\\ v(x,y,t)&=4+4s_y\frac{FD}{\psi^2}-s_y u_s,\qquad X=x-4t,\quad s=s(y).\end{aligned}}{\tag{E4}}
Substitution using (E1)–(E3) gives both continuous DLW equations (1), with \(a=2\) and \(\lambda=-2\). The final expressions divide only by \(\psi\) and \(D\), so zeros of the cosine profile or of \(s_y\) leave the reconstructed fields regular.

 

Initially, \(H=-s\psi\) and \(D=-\psi^2\). Consequently,

 
\paperdisplay{u_0=2sF_0,\qquad v_0=4-6s_yF_0=4-3u_{0y}.}{\tag{E5}}
This calculation explains the joint choice of the two initial fields. The coefficient three is generated by the transformed family. It does not impose a constraint on the subsequent evolution: the DLW equations give \(\partial_t(v-4+3u_y)|_{t=0}=8u_{0xxy}\), which is generally nonzero. At \(y=\pm2\), \(s=0\) gives \(u=0\), while \(s_y=0\) at \(y=0\) gives \(v=4\). The quasi-periodic factors in \(H\) and \(\psi\) cancel in (E4), yielding periodic physical fields.

 

\noindent\textbf{Regularity and reference evaluation.} For the amplitudes \(A=0.05\) and \(A=0.03\), we have \(|s|\le0.025\). Fourier coefficient bounds for (E1), combined with Cauchy estimates on the parameter disk \(|s|\le0.05\), control \(H,\psi\) and their parameter derivatives. Comparing them with \(\psi_0=e^{-sJ_0}\) and \(D_0=-e^{-2sJ_0}\) gives the uniform bounds

 
\paperdisplay{\psi>0.6,\qquad |D|>0.4,\qquad |x|\le96,\quad |y|\le2,\quad 0\le t\le0.01.}{\tag{E6}}
For example, the coefficient estimates \(\|F\|_{\mathcal A_2}<2\), \(\|J_{\rm per}\|_{\mathcal A_2}<11\), and \(0\le C<0.056\), where \(\|q\|_{\mathcal A_2}=\sum_n(1+|\xi_n|)^2|q_n|\), give \(|J_0|<16.432\) on \(|X|\le97\). Integrating (E3) bounds the change in \(\psi\); the forward-heat bounds control the change in \(H\). Cauchy estimates for their \(s\) derivatives then bound the change in \(D\), giving (E6). These lower bounds justify the smooth reconstruction on the full test region.

 

We evaluate (E4) through Fourier series and analytic parameter derivatives. Each heat mode has an explicit time multiplier, so the reference evaluation introduces no time-stepping error. The stored references use a degree-32 exponential expansion and 96 positive Gaussian Fourier modes; the sinusoidal input has one mode. Independent interval evaluation, including analytical tails and floating-point conversion, bounds the error in every stored initial field and time derivative and every stored final field at \(T=0.01\) by \(10^{-13}\). This bound applies at the native sample coordinates used in the error tables.
