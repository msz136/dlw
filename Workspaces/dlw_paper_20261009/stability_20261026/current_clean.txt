##### section-4.1

4.1 Fourier differentiation and spatial formulations
We apply the same Fourier differentiation in
$x$
to Endpoint-SD, Centered-SD and FD. The operators
$\mathcal D_x$
and
$\mathcal D_{xx}$
have multipliers
$\mathrm i\xi$
and
$-\xi^2$
, respectively. The first-derivative multiplier is set to zero at the Nyquist frequency. Products are evaluated at the grid points. These calligraphic operators denote numerical differentiation and are distinct from Hirota's bilinear derivatives.
Both semi-discrete formulations place
$u$
at nodes and
$v$
at edge midpoints; FD places both fields at nodes. Write
$H(u)=u^2/2+2au$
,
$\Delta_hq_k=(q_k-q_{k-1})/h$
,
$\mathcal M_-q_k=(q_k+q_{k-1})/2$
and
$\delta_0q_k=(q_{k+1}-q_{k-1})/(2h)$
. Recalling the physical-field equations (27)–(28), Endpoint-SD takes the form
$\begin{aligned}\Delta_h\dot u_k&=-\mathcal D_x\Delta_hH(u_k)-\mathcal D_{xx}v_k,\\\dot v_k&=-\mathcal D_x[(\mathcal M_-u_k+2a-2h)v_k+hv_k^2/4-4\mathcal M_-u_k-h(\Delta_hu_k)^2/4]-\mathcal D_{xx}\Delta_hu_k.\end{aligned}$
For Centered-SD, the field definition in Section 3.2 gives
$v_k=\Delta_hu_k+2(\omega_k+\omega_{k-1})/h$
. We retain this relation in the numerical formulation. The equations for the time derivatives are
$\begin{aligned}\Delta_h\dot u_k&=-\mathcal D_x\Delta_h[H(u_k)+\omega_k^2/2-h\omega_k]-\mathcal D_{xx}v_k,\\\dot\omega_k&=\mathcal D_{xx}\omega_k-\mathcal D_x[(u_k+2a)\omega_k-hu_k],\\\dot v_k&=\Delta_h\dot u_k+\frac2h(\dot\omega_k+\dot\omega_{k-1}).\end{aligned}$
The first equation is summed from the prescribed lower value of
$\dot u$
. Advancing
$(u,\omega)$
and recovering
$v$
through the field relation implements the physical-field equations of Proposition 3.2. The same differentiated relation supplies
$\dot v$
when measuring the initial spatial error.
For comparison, direct discretization of the continuous DLW system gives the FD baseline,
$\begin{aligned}\Delta_h\dot u_k&=-\mathcal D_x\Delta_hH(u_k)-\mathcal D_{xx}\mathcal M_-v_k,\\\dot v_k&=-\mathcal D_x[(u_k+2a)v_k-4u_k]-\mathcal D_{xx}\delta_0u_k.\end{aligned}$
These equations use the same Fourier operators and lower derivative data as the two semi-discrete formulations. The location of the physical fields and the discrete nonlinear fluxes determine their different spatial approximations.

Taken together, the initial diagnostics in Table 2 and the evolved errors in Table 3 illustrate how the lattice geometry shapes the numerical approximation of the coupled DLW fields. Although Endpoint-SD and Centered-SD originate from the same Hirota–Miwa bilinear reduction and share the same Lax integrability, their physical-field reconstructions behave quite differently in computation. At
$t=0$
, Endpoint-SD shows a smaller residual in the first equation because it avoids the
$O(h^2)$
auxiliary flux term, whereas its one-sided pairing leaves an
$O(h)$
truncation defect in the second equation. Following the evolution to
$T=0.01$
shows that this initial advantage in
$u_t$
is fragile: in the small-amplitude Gaussian and sinusoidal regimes, the mild gradients allow the initial ordering of
$u$
to persist over short times, but in the steeper one- and two-soliton regimes, the first-order defect in
$v$
feeds back into
$u$
through the dispersive coupling term
$v_{xx}$
, causing Endpoint-SD to lose accuracy in both components.
By placing the
$\tau$
functions and bilinear parameters symmetrically, Centered-SD restores second-order consistency to both equations and prevents this cross-component degradation. Across all four test problems—spanning single and interacting solitons as well as localized and periodic dispersive waves—Centered-SD consistently delivers the most accurate approximation of the second field
$v$
, improving upon Endpoint-SD by factors of
$60$
to
$87$
and upon the collocated FD baseline by factors of
$1.7$
to
$3.5$
, while maintaining a competitive second-order approximation in
$u$
. These results confirm that preserving the symmetric staggered structure of the bilinear system translates directly into a more balanced and reliable physical-field solver. With the spatial behavior of the three formulations established, we retain Centered-SD in Sections 5.3 and 5.4 to examine how time integration and adaptive node redistribution affect the computed waves.

$u$

This opposite displacement is a straightforward consequence of adding a first-order temporal defect to a slowly varying spatial residual. In Centered-SD, the state variables
$(u_k,\omega_k)$
are advanced in time and
$v_k$
is recovered from the differentiated staggered relation
$\dot v_k=\Delta_h\dot u_k+\frac2h(\dot\omega_k+\dot\omega_{k-1})$
. Through this relation, the leading
$O(\Delta t)$
truncation error of forward Euler enters the two physical fields with opposite signs, partially canceling the spatial discretization error in
$u$
while adding to it in
$v$
. Confirming this interpretation, if we halve the time step to
$\Delta t=5\times10^{-5}$
, the first-order defect diminishes and the Euler errors move back toward the RK4 benchmark in both components — changing from
$(1.664\times10^{-4},\,8.026\times10^{-5})$
to
$(1.671\times10^{-4},\,7.880\times10^{-5})$
in the one-soliton test, and from
$(1.420\times10^{-4},\,1.090\times10^{-4})$
to
$(1.560\times10^{-4},\,1.048\times10^{-4})$
in the two-soliton test. The smaller
$u$
error of forward Euler in Table 4 is therefore a cancellation between temporal and spatial errors rather than a more accurate resolution of the wave.
Taken together, the terminal table and the time histories clarify three points. First, the rate at which the numerical solution departs from the continuous reference is governed by the spatial discretization: all three integrators share the same growth profile, which remains steady until sharper wave interactions develop. Second, at
$\Delta t=10^{-4}$
, a second-order time integrator already matches the fourth-order benchmark, whereas a first-order scheme introduces a noticeable vertical shift, particularly in the more sensitive second field
$v$
. Third, because temporal and spatial errors can combine with opposite signs in different components, tracking both fields over time is necessary to distinguish genuine accuracy from error cancellation. In Section 5.4, we therefore retain RK4 at
$\Delta t=10^{-4}$
so that temporal errors remain negligible when comparing fixed and adaptive meshes.
5.4 Fixed and adaptive meshes
In our final experiment, we examine how redistributing the shared
$x$
nodes according to the conservation law of Section 4.3 affects the accuracy of Centered-SD. Both the uniform-mesh and adaptive-mesh calculations use the same number of nodes, the same uniform
$y$
lattice (
$h=\Delta x=0.1$
), and the same RK4 time step
$\Delta t=10^{-4}$
up to
$T=0.01$
. To ensure a direct comparison in which node placement is the only difference, both calculations evaluate
$x$
derivatives using the mapped three-point centered difference operators
$D_x$
and
$D_{xx}$
of Section 4.3 (with zero node displacement and unit Jacobian on the uniform grid). Because these local difference operators are less accurate than the spectral Fourier multipliers used in Sections 5.2 and 5.3, the uniform-mesh errors reported below are larger than those in Table 3. We perform this comparison on the one- and two-soliton problems, where the fields decay to zero in the background. In the Gaussian and sinusoidal problems, the second field oscillates about
$v=4$
so that the transverse-averaged conservation density vanishes identically, leaving the equidistribution mapping and mesh velocity of Section 4.3 undefined.
In the adaptive runs, the initial
$x$

$\bar\rho$
computed from the exact initial fields, and the nodes subsequently evolve according to the conservation-based velocity of Section 4.3. The averaged density ranges from
$1.000$
in the background to
$1.150$
across the wave core in the one-soliton case, and from
$1.000$
to
$1.050$
in the wider domain of the two-soliton case. Figure 27 plots the resulting local grid spacing, normalized by the uniform spacing
$\Delta x=0.1$

$t=0$
and
$T=0.01$

In summary, the mesh comparison illustrates both the utility and the natural scope of the conservation-based grid over the present test horizon. With a fixed node count and time integrator, equidistributing the semi-discrete conservation density places additional resolution along the soliton wave band and produces a consistent error reduction in both physical fields. At the same time, because the local difference operators remain second order and the transverse-averaged density varies moderately over the domain, the magnitude of the improvement is bounded by the local spacing compression. Finally, because the wave translates by only a small fraction of a grid cell over
$T=0.01$
, the present experiments primarily test the static adaptation of the grid to the conservation density; longer-time simulations, in which the soliton core travels over many grid spacings, would be required to test the dynamic tracking capability of the moving mesh.
